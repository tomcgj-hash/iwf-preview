01

The two-layer problem

Why does an exhibition center need a different wayfinding model?

A shopping mall changes tenants slowly and a museum changes exhibitions annually. An exhibition center rebuilds its interior on a cycle measured in days. Stand numbering, aisle layout, food court position, seminar rooms and even the direction of the visitor flow all belong to the event and not to the building. A wayfinding system that treats a hall as permanent is wrong by the second day of the build.

So the model has two layers. The base layer holds the building: entrances, halls, service corridors, lifts, toilets, parking, loading docks, all of which do not move. The event layer holds stand polygons, exhibitor records, feature areas, session rooms and sponsor placements, and it is replaced wholesale when the next event loads. Keeping the layers separate is what makes a two to five day turnaround possible at all.

  • Base building layer is stable and surveyed once
  • Event layer is replaced for every event
  • Neither layer edits the other, so neither is lost

02

Event import

How does per-event re-configuration actually work?

The event layer arrives as data: a stand list from the organizer's floor plan export, an exhibitor directory from the registration system, a session program, a sponsor list with tiers. We map the stand polygons to aisles and halls, reconcile numbering conventions, attach exhibitor names and categories, and publish. Where the organizer holds a vector or CAD plan the polygons come across directly, and where the plan is a poster the shapes are traced against the hall grid.

The realistic turnaround is two to five working days for a repeat hall configuration with a clean stand list, and longer for a new hall shape or a directory that arrives as three incompatible spreadsheets. That estimate is worth stating at the contract stage, because the organizer's expectation is usually set by the event build schedule rather than by the mapping work.

  • Stand list, directory, program and sponsor tiers imported
  • Vector plans mapped directly, posters traced to the hall grid
  • Repeat configuration publishes in two to five working days
  • Numbering reconciled before publish, not after the doors open

03

Numbering

How do hall and stand numbers get modelled?

Stand numbering is where most exhibition maps fail. A hall may number by row and position, by an aisle grid, by a scheme inherited from a previous venue, or by a scheme a sponsor requested, and organizers routinely reuse a number a different exhibitor held last month. The map holds the current allocation with an effective date, so a search for a stand returns the exhibitor who is there this week rather than the one who was there in March.

The modeling rules are simple and worth enforcing. Each stand is a polygon inside a hall, each hall has entry points and aisles, and every stand record carries the organizer's code plus any alias. Alias search matters more than it sounds, because visitors arrive with a number from a printed program, a name from a badge, or a booth number quoted verbally by a colleague. Fuzzy matching on exhibitor name and prefix matching on stand code resolves most of these in one search.

  • Stand as polygon, hall as container, aisle as connection
  • Effective dates so last month's allocation cannot leak through
  • Alias codes and fuzzy name matching for messy enquiries
  • Search resolves code, name, category and common misspellings

04

Badge coupling

Can an attendee find an exhibitor from a badge scan?

Badge scanning closes the loop between registration and the map. When a visitor scans at an entrance or at a kiosk, the registration system already knows who they are and often what they registered for. Passing the scanned badge to a kiosk or to the visitor app lets the exhibitor directory open pre-filtered on those interests, and lets a single action send a route to a stand to the visitor's phone or show it on the kiosk screen.

Privacy limits apply and are worth writing down. We read the minimum needed to personalize the session, store no identity in the map platform, and build no individual movement history. Route requests are used to count demand for a hall or a stand, not to profile the person walking it. A reader for RFID, NFC or barcode at the kiosk is an option on the directory terminal.

  • Badge scan filters the directory to registered interests
  • Route to a stand pushed in one action
  • No identity stored in the map platform
  • Demand counted per hall and stand, never per person

05

Sponsorship

Sponsor promotion without cluttering the map

Sponsorship revenue usually comes with visibility promises, and a map is a new surface that has to meet them without turning into an advert. The workable pattern is tiered placement: a sponsor's stand ranks first within its category, carries a badge on the map, and appears in the kiosk advertising loop and on the idle screen, while the route itself stays unaltered. Sponsored ranking is labeled so a visitor can see why it appears first.

The trap is putting a sponsor between a visitor and the stand they searched for. That produces a route nobody trusts, and it damages the exhibitor relationship the sponsorship was meant to support. Sponsored space goes around the route, in ranking, badges, ad loop and session cards, and never inside it.

  • Ranked placement within category, labeled as sponsored
  • Map badge and kiosk ad loop for higher tiers
  • Session cards and feature areas sold as placements
  • Routes never interrupted by a paid stop

06

Day of event

What does the organizer need on the day?

On build day and show day the map is an operational tool as much as a visitor one. A stand changes hands, a session room moves, an aisle closes for a fire inspection, a sponsor withdraws. The organizer needs a person who can edit without waiting for a vendor and a publish path that reaches kiosks and the app within the hour. We hand the editor to the operations lead with a short training session and a change log, so the floor manager can see what moved and when.

07

Footfall data

What does it measure per hall and per stand?

Organizers sell stand space on anticipated footfall, so measured footfall is commercially useful rather than merely interesting. Aggregate heatmaps show which halls and aisles draw people, which feature areas work as anchors, where visitors enter, and how many searched for exhibitors in a category that returned no match. That last number is the most actionable, because a category with high search and zero results is a gap in the floor plan for next year.

The same data supports staffing, cleaning and catering schedules, and gives the venue operational evidence when it negotiates the next event's hall allocation. The privacy model does not change between events: counts, zones and hours, with no identification of individuals and no camera-based identity tracking.

  • Heatmaps by hall, aisle and hour
  • Unmatched searches expose catalog gaps
  • Evidence for hall allocation in the next contract
  • Aggregate only, no individual identification

08

Turnaround

How fast can a new event map go live?

The question organizers ask first is how fast a map can be live. With the base building already mapped, a repeat hall configuration publishes in two to five working days from a clean stand list, and a first-time hall or a new numbering scheme takes longer because the polygons and the numbering have to be reconciled. The base building itself is a one-off: survey three to five days, map production two to three weeks, setup and integration two to four weeks.

Two things decide whether it takes days or weeks. A stand list in machine-readable form rather than a poster, and a named person at the organizer who can approve the numbering before publish. When both exist, an event's map can be built, checked and live inside the build window, which is the only deadline that matters.

  • Base building mapped once, typically 6-10 weeks
  • Repeat event layer in two to five working days
  • Machine-readable stand list shortens the cycle most
  • A named numbering approver prevents the last delay

FAQ

Common questions

Can't see your question? Send it with your floor count and we answer within one working day.

Ask a specific question

How quickly can the map change for a new event?

With the building already mapped, a repeat hall configuration publishes in two to five working days once we have a clean stand list. A new hall shape or an unfamiliar numbering scheme takes longer because polygons and numbering must be reconciled first. Day-of edits, such as a stand change or a closed aisle, can be published within the day by the organizer's operations lead.

Can visitors search an exhibitor by name or stand number?

Both, plus category and common aliases. Each stand keeps its organizer code and any alias, so a number printed in last month's program still resolves to the right hall position. Exhibitor name search uses fuzzy matching, which handles the spelling variations and abbreviation that visitors type at a kiosk under time pressure.

Does it integrate with badge scanning and registration?

At the session level, yes. A kiosk can carry an RFID, NFC or barcode reader, and a scanned badge opens the exhibitor directory filtered to that visitor's registered interests, with a route to a stand sent in one action. The map platform stores no identity and builds no personal movement history, and no data is written back to the registration system.

How does sponsor promotion appear on the map?

Through placement around the route rather than inside it: ranked first within a category with a visible sponsored label, a badge on the map pin, a slot in the kiosk advertising loop and on the idle screen, and session cards where relevant. The visitor's route to a stand they searched for is never interrupted by a paid stop, which is what keeps the map trusted.

Can the organizer edit the layout without the vendor?

Yes. The operations lead receives the map editor with a short training session and a change log, so stands, session rooms, closures and notices can be edited and republished on the day. Structural remapping of a new hall still needs survey work, but the changes that happen during a build window do not.

What does it measure per hall and per stand?

Aggregate heatmaps by hall, aisle and hour, plus search counts per category and per exhibitor, unmatched searches, and entry zone usage. That supports stand pricing, staffing and next year's floor plan. No individual attendee is identified and no route is linked to a badge or a registration.

Can it handle several halls, outdoor areas and a congress wing?

Yes. Halls, outdoor exhibition areas, the congress wing and the connections between them are modelled as one network with timed edges, so a route can run from a car park through a hall into a session room across a link corridor. Multi-level halls and mezzanine stands are supported, with up to 20 levels per building.
