PEOPLE COUNTING

People counting system: what to count, and how to count it

A people counting system measures how many people enter a building or zone, how long they stay and where they go. We combine fixed sensor counters at doorways with behavior data from the venue map: searches, route requests and destinations reached. You get visitor counts, dwell time, zone occupancy and footfall heatmaps.

Direct answer

  • Boundary counts plus in-venue journey data
  • Zone occupancy and dwell by floor
  • Anonymised, aggregate reports for tenants

01

Three questions

What does a people counting system actually count?

A people counting system answers three different questions, and they are usually mixed into one number by accident. How many people entered. How long they stayed. Where they went inside. The first is a flow count at a boundary. The second is dwell, measured inside a zone. The third is either a zone occupancy series or a path. A venue that needs all three is buying three data streams, and they have to agree on time windows or the reports will contradict each other in front of a tenant.

The boundary matters more than the sensor. In a shopping center the boundary is the street entrances and the car park lift lobbies, not the building shell. In a hospital it is the main entrance plus each clinic waiting area, because queue behavior happens there. In an exhibition hall it is each hall door, since the organiser is asked for occupancy per hall rather than a whole-site total.

We treat sensor counts and map behavior as complementary evidence. A doorway counter tells you that 4,000 people came through the east entrance between ten and eleven. The map tells you that 1,200 of the visitors who used it searched for a named tenant, that 380 requested a route and did not arrive, and that the elevator bank was the point where most of them stopped. One number is a fact about the door. The other is a fact about the visit.

02

Methods

Which counting method fits your building?

There are four practical ways to count people indoors, plus behavior data from the wayfinding layer. They differ in what they measure, what they cost to run, and how much governance they demand. Most venues end up with a mix: sensors at the few boundaries that matter, and behavior data everywhere the map is used. The table below is the short version of a decision that usually takes one meeting, provided the venue states what the count must answer and who will read the result.

Be sceptical of a figure quoted without a method. A doorway counter and a Wi-Fi estimate will rarely agree, and neither is wrong: they are counting different populations on different clocks. Ask which population a number describes, and over what window, before you compare it with last year. A method stated in the report is worth more than a fourth decimal place.

03

Metrics

Which metrics are worth reporting?

Six metrics cover most operational and commercial questions. Everything else is a derivative of them, and adding more columns to a dashboard usually reduces the number of people who read it. The list below is deliberately short, and each row names the decision it supports, because a metric with no decision attached is a number someone will eventually stop maintaining.

Define each one in writing before the first report goes out, and state the window it covers. A dwell figure of 18 minutes means nothing until you say whether it is a median, whether it counts staff, and whether it excludes people who passed through in under a minute.

04

Heatmap

What does a footfall heatmap show that a total does not?

A footfall heatmap colors the floor plan by density of presence, floor by floor, with a time filter. It shows the shape of use: which corridor is the spine, which wing dies after six in the evening, where people stop rather than walk. A total of 30,000 visitors can hide the fact that one wing receives 4 percent of them.

Heatmaps come from two feeds. Route density from the map shows where people asked to go and walked. Zone occupancy from sensors or gateways shows where bodies actually were. Overlaying both is the useful step, because the gap between intent and presence is where the operational problem usually sits: a signed route that people abandon, a lift lobby that becomes a bottleneck at the same hour every day.

What a heatmap cannot tell you is why. It will not distinguish a busy dead end from a busy destination without a second layer of data, and it will not show service corridors where nobody carries a phone. Treat it as a map of symptoms. The diagnosis needs route completion rates and, for the commercial questions, tenant-level reporting.

05

Paths

Can you follow a journey without identifying a person?

Yes, if the identifier is short lived and separated from anything that names a person. A journey path is a sequence of zones and timestamps attached to a rotating pseudonymous key. It answers questions about routes and abandonment. It does not answer questions about who.

In practice the path view is used for four questions. Where do visitors enter relative to where they intend to go. Which junction causes the most route restarts. How long is the gap between entering and reaching the first destination. Which destinations are reached by people who arrived from the car park rather than the street. Those four answers usually change signage and directory placement in the first quarter.

The commercial value is in the funnel rather than the individual line. If 1,200 people search for a category, 900 request a route and 640 arrive, the interesting number is the 260 who gave up. A heatmap would show the crowd; the path view shows the drop.

06

Commercial

How do you use counts in tenant reporting and rents?

Footfall affects rent because it supports the argument about passing trade. A tenant asking for a turnover-based clause, or a landlord defending a service charge, will both point at counts. That makes the counting method a commercial document, not an IT detail.

Three practices keep the numbers usable in a negotiation. Publish the method: sensor type, position, deduplication window and what is excluded. Report ranges when the method is an estimate, and say so. Keep a stable baseline, because a system change mid-lease resets every trend line and invites a dispute.

Tenant-level reporting should stay aggregate. A tenant can receive footfall past their frontage, the share of visitors who reached their zone, and dwell in the adjacent corridor. They should not receive identifiable visitor data, and the lease should say that plainly rather than leaving it to the data protection schedule.

07

Privacy

What does anonymisation require in practice?

Anonymisation is a process, not a setting. It means the identifiers used to link events are hashed, rotated on a fixed schedule, and never joined to a name, a phone number, a membership record or a payment record. It also means retention is short enough that a path cannot be reconstructed after the fact.

We recommend a published notice at each entrance, a retention window stated in days, and aggregate-only exports for marketing and leasing teams. Camera based counting, where it is used, is a separate decision with its own signage and policy. We do not build identity tracking into the counting layer, and we do not offer face recognition.

For venues in regulated sectors, the safe design is the boring one: count at boundaries with sensors, use map behavior for journey questions, keep the two datasets in separate tables, and let only aggregate views leave the platform. That design is also easier to explain to a data protection officer, because each table has one purpose and one retention rule, and neither can be joined to a membership record by accident.

FAQ

Common questions

Can't see your question? Send it with your floor count and we answer within one working day.

Ask a specific question

Do we need sensors if we already have a wayfinding app?

If you only need to know where app users go, no. Map behavior gives route requests and arrivals for that population. If you need a headcount for safety limits, rent negotiation or staffing, you need boundary sensors, because app users are a minority of visitors and their share changes over time.

How accurate is indoor people counting?

It depends on the method and the doorway. A well mounted directional sensor on a single clear doorway is the most reliable option. Camera analytics lose accuracy in dense crowds and where doorways are partly occluded. Wi-Fi estimates are affected by address randomisation and device mix, so we report them as trends rather than headcounts.

Can counts be reported per tenant?

Yes, as aggregates: footfall past a frontage, the share of visitors who entered a zone, and dwell in the adjacent corridor. We do not expose identifiable visitor data to tenants, and we recommend the lease states the method and the reporting window so the numbers remain usable in a rent discussion.

What is the difference between a heatmap and a journey path?

A heatmap shows density of presence over a period, so it shows where people stood. A journey path shows the order of zones visited by one pseudonymous key, so it shows where a visit broke down. Heatmaps are better for layout and signage questions, paths are better for abandonment and funnel questions.

Does the system store video or images?

The counting layer does not need to. Thermal and depth sensors output counts, not pictures. Where a venue already runs camera analytics for other reasons, that footage sits in the venue's own video system under the venue's own policy, and our reports consume counts only.

How long should counts be retained?

Long enough for a seasonal comparison, short enough that paths cannot be rebuilt later. Most venues keep daily aggregates indefinitely and individual journey records for a short window stated in days. The retention setting is per venue, and automatic deletion is part of the platform rather than a manual task.

Can we count visitors in the car park as well?

Yes. Parking levels are drawn as zones like any other, and where the parking system exposes plate recognition or barrier events those can be used as a second boundary count. That gives you a cross-check between vehicles entering and people arriving on the retail floors.

What do we need to provide before a counting project starts?

Floor plans for every counted level, a list of the boundaries that matter, and a decision on what the reports must answer. Sensor mounting positions are confirmed during the survey, which typically takes three to five days for a site of 50,000 to 100,000 m2.
