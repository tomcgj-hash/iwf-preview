PATROL SYSTEM

Patrol system: routes, checkpoints and compliance you can show

A patrol system turns a walking route into a set of verifiable tasks. You define ordered checkpoints, officers check in by code scan, tag tap or kiosk reader, and the platform reports completion rate, duration and missed points. Incidents reported during a patrol stay attached to the checkpoint where they occurred.

Direct answer

  • Ordered routes with proof-of-presence checkpoints
  • Missed checkpoint alerts during the shift
  • Completion, duration and incident reporting

01

Framing

Why treat patrol as a task system rather than surveillance?

Patrol fails in two directions. If it is unverified, nobody can show that it happened, and a single missed night becomes a liability question with no answer. If it is treated as surveillance, officers work to the tracker rather than to the building, and the useful observations stop being written down.

A task system sits between the two. The route is a list of things that must be checked: a fire door, a plant room, a car park level, a loading dock. Each checkpoint produces a timestamp. The officer's judgement fills the rest, and the incident report is the output that matters most. Proving a walk happened is necessary; knowing what was seen on the walk is the point.

That framing also decides what not to build. We do not treat patrol as continuous position monitoring of an individual, and we do not score officers on distance walked. The measures are completion, duration and reported incidents, because those are the three that change behavior in the right direction.

02

Setup

How are routes and checkpoints defined?

A route is an ordered list of checkpoints, each attached to a point on the venue map, with an expected time window and a tolerance. Routes can be scheduled by shift, by weekday and by season, and a site usually needs between four and a dozen of them: perimeter, retail floors after close, plant and roof, car park, loading dock.

Checkpoints come in three practical forms. A printed code scanned by the officer's handset is the cheapest and easiest to add, since it is a label on a wall. An NFC or BLE tag is more durable and harder to fake, and it works when the camera is not the operating mode. A directory kiosk reader, where a kiosk sits on the route, gives a fixed check-in point with a screen that can show the next task.

The design rule is one checkpoint per decision point, not one per ten meters. If a checkpoint is placed where nothing can go wrong, it becomes a button press in a corridor and the officer learns to press it early. Place them where the building is weakest: fire doors, plant access, dark corners of the car park.

  • Ordered checkpoint lists with expected windows and tolerance
  • Printed code scan, NFC or BLE tag, or kiosk reader check-in
  • Routes scheduled by shift, weekday and season
  • Checkpoints placed at weak points, not at equal intervals

03

Alerts

What happens when a checkpoint is missed?

A missed checkpoint raises a flag at the end of its window, not at the moment it is due. The supervisor sees the route, the completed points and the gap, and can contact the officer or reassign the remainder. Escalation is configurable: a first reminder to the officer, then the shift lead, then the duty manager if the gap is on a critical route.

Good deployments separate critical and routine checkpoints. A fire door left unchecked at close is worth an immediate escalation. A lift lobby checklist running twenty minutes late is not, and treating the two the same trains supervisors to ignore both. Set the escalation by checkpoint, not by route.

The same mechanism handles the legitimate exceptions. An officer diverted to an incident, a checkpoint blocked by a delivery, a route paused for a cleaning crew: the platform records the reason against the route so the completion figure is not damaged by events outside the officer's control. Without that field, officers invent workarounds, and the data becomes fiction.

04

Reporting

Which compliance numbers are worth reporting?

Three numbers cover the operational question. Completion rate is completed checkpoints divided by scheduled checkpoints over a period. Duration is the elapsed time for a route against its planned window. Frequency is how often the route actually ran against how often it was scheduled.

Report them by route and by week, not by officer. Officer-level league tables produce exactly the behavior the system was meant to prevent: rushed walks, early check-ins and unreported problems. A route that consistently runs at 60 percent completion has a design problem, and the fix is usually to shorten it or move the checkpoints.

Duration deserves a second look because it is easy to misread. A route that suddenly takes half the time is more suspicious than one that runs long. A route that runs long every night usually has a bottleneck the operations team does not know about, which is a useful finding in itself.

05

Incident reporting

How does an officer report an incident during a patrol?

The report is filed from the same handset, attached to the checkpoint where it happened, with a category, a short description, optional photos and a severity. Categories are venue specific but usually cover fire safety, security, cleaning, maintenance and safety hazards. The report is timestamped against the checkpoint, which is what makes it credible in a review.

Routing is the part that decides whether the system is used. A leaking ceiling should reach facilities, a propped fire door should reach the duty manager, and a broken light should join a maintenance queue. Each category gets a default owner, and the officer should not have to choose a department at eleven at night.

Reports also feed the route design. When the same checkpoint generates reports every week, the checkpoint is doing its job and the underlying asset needs a repair. When a route generates no reports for a quarter, either the building is in good order or the check is being pressed without looking, and the two are worth distinguishing.

06

Review

How do you improve a patrol after the first month?

Wait a month before changing anything. The first two weeks produce a backlog of missed checkpoints caused by settings rather than behavior: tags not yet commissioned, windows set too tight, a route that has never actually been walked end to end. Adjusting the plan inside that window just moves the noise from one checkpoint to another and teaches officers that the route is provisional.

Then review in this order. First, drop checkpoints that can never reveal a problem. Second, move checkpoints that are always missed because they sit behind a locked door. Third, shorten any route that consistently runs over its window, since a route that cannot be finished in the allotted time will be finished in a hurry. Fourth, retune escalation so critical points and routine points behave differently.

Finally, connect patrol to the rest of the building. A patrol that finishes at a plant room where an alarm was raised two hours earlier should not need a separate enquiry. Linking checkpoints to alarm zones and to access control events is what turns a checklist into an operations record.

FAQ

Common questions

Can't see your question? Send it with your floor count and we answer within one working day.

Ask a specific question

Can officers check in with their own phone?

Yes, if the venue app is installed and the officer consents to the tracking state it displays. An issued handset or badge is the cleaner arrangement because consent and device management sit in one place, and it avoids personal device policy arguments during a shift.

Does a patrol system need indoor positioning?

No. Check-in works on its own and produces the compliance record. Positioning adds the trail between checkpoints, which is useful for incident review and for explaining a gap. Many venues start with check-in only and add position later if a specific question needs it.

How do you stop officers checking in early?

Place checkpoints at physical weak points rather than convenient corridors, use tags that need proximity, and require the checkpoint order. A route that requires sequence and proximity cannot be completed from a staff room. The stronger fix is behavioural: report by route, not by officer.

What is a realistic completion rate?

A well designed route is achievable, so shortfalls usually indicate a design problem rather than indiscipline. We look at whether the plan fits the roster before judging the walk. Routes that consistently run below their plan are shortened or re-checkpointed rather than escalated.

Can patrol routes change by season or event?

Yes. Routes are scheduled by date range, so a winter route that adds the car park and a summer route that adds the roof terrace can coexist. Event routes for exhibition halls work the same way and expire automatically after the show.

How are incidents followed up?

Each category has a default owner and a queue. The report carries the checkpoint, the time and any photos, so the owner sees the context without asking. Closing the report is recorded, which gives the venue a defensible log for safety inspections.

What evidence does the system produce for an audit?

A route instance record with every checkpoint timestamp, any exceptions with reasons, and the incident reports filed. That is the package a fire safety or insurance auditor usually asks for. Position history is optional and separately retained, so a venue can produce the audit record without exposing a trail of individual movement.
