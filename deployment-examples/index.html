01

Framing

What these profiles are, and what they are not

A reference profile is a sizing exercise: the same modules, tenants and floors arranged the way that building type actually behaves, so a buyer can see which cost lines move. It is not a case study. We do not publish client names, project photographs or performance percentages without written permission, and we would rather show a defensible configuration than a logo wall.

Each profile below states the building, what gets installed, what is genuinely difficult about that shape, and what the operator can measure afterwards. The difficulty section is the useful part: it is where budgets usually move.

02

Profile A

Profile A: a large single-level underground retail complex

This is the hardest shape for wayfinding and the one most often sold short. One continuous floor plate of roughly 55,000 m2, no daylight, a ceiling around 4.2 m, and more than 30 themed street zones that tenants swap between seasons. There are no floor changes to help a visitor build a mental model, so orientation has to come from zone naming, landmarks and the map itself.

The configuration that fits: about 40 BLE gateways along the main circulation loops, 14 directory kiosks at street-zone junctions and vertical connections, AR wayfinding enabled because the corridor views are visually repetitive, four languages, and integrations to the parking system and the venue app. Positioning is deliberately dense at junctions, not uniform across the plate.

  • Hard part one: a single level gives no vertical cue, so routes must be written around named zones, not distances.
  • Hard part two: a 4.2 m ceiling limits gateway mounting height and line of sight between loops.
  • Hard part three: seasonal tenant churn in themed zones means the map editor matters more than the hardware.
  • Measured afterwards: searches per zone, route completion rate, kiosk use per junction, dwell by street zone.

03

Profile B

Profile B: a five-level city mall with two parking levels

The familiar mixed-use mall: roughly 96,000 m2 across five retail levels and two parking levels, 240 tenants, two food courts, a cinema on the top floor and a lift core at each end. The wayfinding problem is dominated by vertical movement and by the car: a visitor who cannot find the bay has not finished the visit.

Configuration: 60 to 80 BLE gateways across the retail levels, 12 kiosks at entrances and lift lobbies, reverse car finding by plate number or bay number with the parking system's plate recognition, patrol check-in at 18 checkpoints, and integrations to CRM, parking and the signage CMS. The map carries two parking floors with bay-level points of interest.

  • Hard part one: floor detection at stairwells and escalator landings, where a wrong floor ruins the route.
  • Hard part two: parking plate recognition interfaces vary enormously between suppliers, so that integration is scoped early.
  • Hard part three: promotions and event content change weekly; the kiosk and app must read the same source.
  • Measured afterwards: route completion, reverse-car sessions per 1,000 visitors, patrol completion rate, top destinations.

Planning values for this profile, confirmed after survey.

04

Profile C

Profile C: a hospital campus with seven buildings

A 900-bed campus: outpatient tower, four clinic blocks, a diagnostics building and two parking structures. Visitors arrive anxious, often for the first time, usually with an appointment time and a department name rather than a building name. Wrong-building arrivals are the operational cost this profile targets.

Configuration: hybrid positioning, Wi-Fi for the covered campus walkways and BLE inside the clinic blocks where Wi-Fi is patchy, 24 kiosks split between entrances and department waiting areas, appointment QR routing that opens the route to the correct department, step-free priority by default, three languages, and a read-only directory feed from the hospital information system.

  • Hard part one: department names change more often than buildings, so naming has to be owned by the hospital, not the integrator.
  • Hard part two: accessibility is not a toggle for a hospital, it is the default route.
  • Hard part three: patient movement data must be aggregated at collection; no per-person trail is acceptable.
  • Measured afterwards: kiosk searches by department, accessibility route share, wrong-building arrivals reported at reception.

Comparison

How the three profiles differ in cost shape

The same modules do not cost the same in these three buildings. Underground retail is gateway-dense and kiosk-light per square meter, the city mall is kiosk-heavy and integration-heavy, and the hospital spends on kiosks, languages and accessibility routing rather than on AR.

06

Adaptation

What changes when we size it for your building

Four quantities decide most of the scope: mapped area, number of levels, number of points of interest, and the number of surfaces plus integrations. A fifth decides the schedule: how current the floor plans are. When a venue sends us survey-grade drawings, map production compresses; when it sends a scanned lease plan from 2014, it does not.

The adjustments we usually make: fewer gateways where a level is short and open, more kiosks where footfall concentrates at two or three entrances, AR only where corridors are visually repetitive, and a staged go-live where the map and directory ship before positioning calibration.

  • Single-level and open plan: fewer gateways, more zone naming discipline.
  • Many small levels: more vertical cores to instrument and more floor-detection work.
  • High tenant churn: invest in the editor and in the content owner's training, not in more hardware.
  • Strict data rules: on-premise delivery, which changes the deployment plan but not the module list.

07

Boundaries

What we will not do with a reference profile

We will not attach a client logo to a profile it does not belong to, quote a percentage improvement we did not measure, or present a planning configuration as a delivered project. If you need a named reference for a tender, we will tell you what can be disclosed and arrange a reference call where the client agrees to it.

The same rule applies to the numbers in the tables above: they are sizing values. After the survey, the hardware schedule, the accuracy target and the acceptance test are written into the contract, and those are the numbers we are held to.

FAQ

Common questions

Can't see your question? Send it with your floor count and we answer within one working day.

Ask a specific question

Are these real projects?

No. They are sizing profiles built from the building types the platform is deployed in, with parameters chosen to show where cost moves. We do not publish client names or results without written permission, so a profile is the honest alternative to an invented case study.

Why is there no airport or exhibition center profile?

Because a profile should reflect a shape we can size credibly. Airports and exhibition centers are supported by the same platform, but their procurement and integration patterns differ enough that we would rather scope them in a conversation than publish a configuration we cannot defend line by line.

Which profile is closest to a normal shopping mall?

Profile B. A single-level mall with no parking under management behaves more like Profile A: fewer vertical cores, more emphasis on zone naming and directory content, and less hardware than the five-level example.

Can we mix profiles?

Yes, and most projects do. A mall with a hospital-style clinic floor, or a campus with a retail podium, takes the modules it needs from each profile. The sizing follows the building, not the label.

How much does each profile cost?

We do not publish amounts, because the same profile can differ by a wide margin depending on existing network, kiosk specification and integration depth. Send the area, levels and points of interest and we return a budgetary proposal within 24 hours.

What do we need to send for a scoped comparison?

Current floor plans, the number of levels including parking, an approximate tenant or department count, the systems you want connected, and any deadline or tender date. With those five items we can place your building against the closest profile and show the differences.

Do you provide a specification for a tender?

Yes. We can issue a technical specification with scope, positioning design, interface list and acceptance criteria that you can put out to other bidders, including the accuracy target you intend to enforce.
