AR NAVIGATION

AR indoor navigation: camera overlay on the real corridor

AR indoor navigation draws the route on the phone camera view, so the arrow sits on the actual corridor instead of a flat plan. It works best over the last short stretch at a junction where a 2D map is ambiguous, and depends on anchoring: visual tracking, beacon positioning or both, against a surveyed map.

01

Definition

What is AR indoor navigation?

AR indoor navigation is a camera view of the corridor in front of you, with a route path, an arrow and the distance to the next turn drawn into that view. It is not a game and not a novelty filter. The render is tied to the phone's position and heading, so the path bends around the corner you can actually see, and the instruction reads as turn left at the pharmacy rather than as a compass bearing and a corridor number.

The distinction that matters commercially is that AR is a mode inside a navigation system, not a replacement for it. The map, the routing engine and the positioning layer do the work. The camera view is a presentation of the same route, offered when the geometry ahead is hard to describe, and switched off when it adds nothing. A visitor who declines the camera still gets the full route, which is the point of keeping the mode separate from the routing engine.

02

Mechanics

How does camera overlay routing actually work?

Three things have to agree: where the phone is, which way the phone is facing, and where the route runs in the building. Position comes from visual tracking against a map of the site, from beacon or Wi-Fi ranging, or from a combination of both. Orientation comes from the device sensors plus the camera frame, because a magnetometer alone drifts badly near lift shafts, escalators, escalator motors and steel risers, and a wrong heading is the most obvious AR failure a visitor can see.

Visual tracking, sometimes called SLAM, compares camera features with a prepared map of the corridor and refines both position and heading. It needs a prepared map: feature data per zone, captured during a calibration walk. Beacon-anchored tracking uses fixed gateways for the absolute position and the camera only for orientation, which is cheaper to prepare and more stable in corridors with little texture, uniform lighting or repeated storefronts where visual matching can confuse one stretch with another.

Off-route correction is the part that decides whether the mode is trusted. If the walker turns early, stops at a lift or walks twenty meters past a junction, the system detects the divergence, redraws from the current position and says so plainly, rather than animating a path that runs along a wall beside them. That behavior, not the graphic quality, is what separates a usable AR mode from a demo clip.

03

Trade-offs

When does AR beat a 2D map, and when does it not?

AR wins at the last thirty meters. A junction where four corridors look identical, a lift lobby with three possible exits, a concourse where the unit number is hidden behind a column: that is where a camera view removes doubt, and where a 2D plan forces the visitor to translate a dot into a physical space. It also wins for visitors who are already walking and holding the phone, because it needs no reading of a plan and no re-orientation.

It loses almost everywhere else. A 2D map is faster to glance at, works in a crowd where the camera view is blocked by shoulders, works in a pocket with the phone half-drawn, and consumes nothing like the same battery. For a route of three hundred meters across two floors, a map with a clear floor transition is better in almost every case. Presenting AR as the default is a design error dressed as innovation.

The honest configuration is AR as a fallback aid: the 2D map is the default, AR is offered at the decision points the venue knows are confusing. That keeps the battery cost bounded, keeps the operating system permission prompt as a deliberate choice, and means a visitor who declines the camera is not cut off from navigation. A visitor whose phone cannot track at all, or who has no signal, still receives the same route on the map, so the mode stays optional at every step.

  • Use AR at low-visibility junctions and lift lobbies
  • Keep the 2D map as the default view
  • Do not offer AR for long straight walks
  • Test with a crowd, not in an empty corridor
  • Measure whether AR routes complete more often than mapped ones

04

Fit

Which venues benefit from AR wayfinding?

Venues with repeated, hard-to-describe geometry benefit most: shopping centers with symmetrical concourses, airports and rail terminals with parallel piers and anonymous gates, hospitals with long department corridors, exhibition halls where stand numbers are not visible from the aisle, and campus buildings where rooms share a numbering pattern across several blocks. Fit is judged per walking path rather than per venue, because one building can have two corridors that justify AR and twenty that do not.

Venues with clear, nameable paths benefit least. A small clinic with one corridor, an office lobby with one lift bank and a single destination, or a museum with a single linear route does not need a camera overlay. In those cases a directory kiosk plus a printed map answers the question at a fraction of the effort, and AR becomes a specification item that scores well in a proposal and is never opened.

Judge fit by the number of ambiguous junctions on the walking paths, not by the size of the building.

05

Constraints

What about device support, battery and privacy?

Device support splits into in-app and in-browser delivery. An in-app AR mode can use the full camera and sensor stack and is consistent across the fleet of phones your visitors actually carry. A browser-based mode reaches visitors without an install, which matters for a mall or an airport, but depends on the phone and browser combination and needs a graceful fallback to the map when the camera or motion sensors are unavailable.

Battery is the constraint nobody plans for. Camera plus sensors plus rendering is the heaviest combination on a phone, so AR should run in short bursts at decision points rather than for the whole walk. Off-route correction, screen dimming between instructions and stopping the camera when the phone is lowered all reduce the load. Tell visitors the mode uses the camera; do not let them discover it from a warm pocket.

Privacy is a design choice, and the defensible one is simple: frames are processed on the device for position and guidance, not stored and not sent for identification. There is no face detection, no biometric identification and no image retention. If a venue wants analytics, use route and search events, not camera frames. Put that processing condition in the contract, with a plain statement of where the camera stream goes and where it does not.

06

Deployment

What content and calibration does AR routing need?

AR needs everything a normal indoor navigation project needs, plus preparation per zone. The base is the 3D multi-floor map with signed floor drawings and a stable tenant or department list. On top of that, each zone where AR will be offered needs a calibration walk: capture, feature preparation for visual tracking, and a heading check at the junctions. Where beacons are used instead, the work is gateway placement and a survey rather than feature capture.

Content is the underestimated item. An AR route is authored, not generated: the path is drawn on the map, the junction instructions are written, and the reference stills are captured under the lighting the visitor will meet. That lighting changes, so a route captured in an empty corridor at eight in the morning reads differently at noon with shutters open and a crowd in the frame. Plan a review pass after the first month of live use.

The rollout does not need to cover the whole building. Start with the junctions the directory data flags as most queried, measure completion rates there, then extend to the next set. That keeps the preparation cost proportional to the benefit and gives you evidence before committing to full coverage. A first pass over three to six zones usually produces a clearer case than a coverage plan for the whole building.

  • 3D map and signed drawings as the base layer
  • Calibration walk per zone, with heading check at junctions
  • Route authoring per path, written junction instructions
  • Reference stills captured under real lighting
  • Review pass after the first month of live use

FAQ

Common questions

Can't see your question? Send it with your floor count and we answer within one working day.

Ask a specific question

Does AR indoor navigation need beacons to work?

No. Visual tracking can carry the mode on its own in zones with enough texture, and it needs no hardware in the building. Most venues still specify beacons or Wi-Fi positioning so the map view works everywhere and AR can fall back to a fixed position anchor in corridors with uniform walls, repeated storefronts or heavy footfall.

Which phones can run the AR mode?

Recent iOS and Android devices with a working camera and motion sensors. Rather than publishing a device list that ages badly, we gate the mode at runtime: if the phone cannot track reliably, the visitor stays on the map view with the same route. In-app delivery is more consistent than browser delivery across a mixed fleet.

How much battery does the camera view use?

Enough that it should not run for a whole walk. Camera, sensors and rendering together are the heaviest combination on the phone. Running AR in short bursts at decision points, pausing the camera between instructions and dimming the screen keeps the cost close to a normal navigation session rather than turning the phone into a heater.

Can AR replace the floor plan entirely?

No, and it should not be sold that way. A flat map is faster to glance at, works in a crowd and needs no camera permission. AR is a fallback aid at junctions where the map is ambiguous. Venues that make AR the only view usually find visitors switching back within a session, and the mode ends up unused.

What happens when the camera view does not match the map?

The mode detects that tracking has degraded and returns to the map view with the route preserved, rather than drawing a path that no longer corresponds to the corridor. Corridors with uniform texture, temporary hoardings or heavy crowds are the usual causes. Authoring those zones with beacon anchoring instead removes most of the problem.

Do you store camera images from visitors?

No. Frames are processed on the device to establish position and draw the route. They are not stored, not uploaded and not used for identification. There is no face detection and no biometric processing. Analytics come from route and search events, which is also the data a privacy review is likely to accept.

How much content does AR authoring add to a project?

Per zone: a calibration walk, feature preparation or beacon survey, a written instruction per junction and reference stills under real lighting. Budget it per junction rather than per floor, since a level with two decision points costs a fraction of a level with eight. Zones are added incrementally, so the first release can cover only the junctions that generate the most queries.
