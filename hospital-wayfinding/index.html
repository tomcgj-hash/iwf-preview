01

The arrival problem

Why does hospital wayfinding fail before the corridor?

A patient arriving for a 09:20 appointment usually holds three pieces of information: a department name, a clinician name and a floor number that may be out of date. What they do not hold is the building. Hospital campuses grow by accretion, so cardiology can sit in a 1970s block while the entrance named on the appointment letter belongs to an outpatient center four hundred meters away. The person walks to the wrong desk, queues, and is redirected. The slot is lost and the clinic absorbs the delay for the rest of the morning.

Signage cannot fix this, because signage is static. A printed board cannot know that today's dermatology clinic moved to level 2 while the floor list still says level 4. Directory kiosks and a phone route can, provided the map underneath is maintained as operational data rather than as a design deliverable. That maintenance question, not the screen hardware, is where most hospital projects succeed or fail.

  • Campus arrival, not corridor navigation, is the usual failure point
  • Appointment letters and printed boards go stale at different rates
  • Route accuracy depends on map maintenance, not screen quality

02

Routing model

How does appointment and department routing work?

We read the department directory from the hospital information system on a read-only basis: department names, locations, opening hours and the mapping between clinic codes and physical rooms. Nothing is written back, and no patient record is queried. That directory becomes the POI layer of the map, so when a clinic code changes room the route changes with it after the next sync. Read-only access is a deliberate limit. It keeps the integration inside a data protection review that most hospitals can complete in weeks rather than quarters.

On the visitor side the route arrives through three channels that share one map: a link in the appointment message, a QR poster at each entrance, and a directory kiosk in the main concourse. The message link is the strongest of the three because it carries context. It can open already aimed at the right building, the right floor and the right clinic, with the check-in desk marked as an intermediate stop rather than only the final door. A visitor running late can see walking time before leaving the car park.

  • Clinic codes map to physical rooms through one directory
  • Message link arrives pre-aimed at building, floor and clinic
  • Check-in desk marked as a stop, not just the end door
  • No write access and no patient record query on the integration

03

Route types

Which routes does an outpatient actually need?

A hospital map is not one map. The same building needs at least four route families, and they disagree with each other about what counts as a good route. A visitor heading to a ward wants the shortest path. A post-surgical patient wants the path with the fewest floor changes. A porter wants the service corridor. A fire warden wants egress only. Modeling these as route profiles on one dataset, rather than as separate apps, keeps the maintenance load to a single map.

04

Privacy model

How do you keep patient movement private?

We do not identify anyone. People counting and heatmaps work from search queries, route requests and anonymous sensor counts: how many people searched for a department, how many asked for a route, which entrances were used at what hour. There is no camera-based identity tracking, no facial recognition, no device fingerprinting and no link between a route and a patient record. The output is aggregate demand, not individual movement, and that distinction is written into the deployment rather than left to policy.

Staff positioning is the one place where an individual can be located, and it is scoped tightly. It runs on consenting staff badges or staff handsets during a shift, for dispatch, portering, patrol check-in and emergency response. It is not used to monitor visitors or patients and it does not read clinical systems. Where a hospital wants staff location at all, we recommend publishing the scope in the staff handbook before go-live, because the technical limit is easy to state and the cultural one needs saying out loud.

  • Counting is aggregate, per zone and per hour
  • No camera identity, no facial recognition, no device fingerprinting
  • Staff location only on consenting badges, only during shifts

05

Change control

Signage replacement logic for a working hospital

Department moves are constant in a working hospital: a clinic is relocated for refurbishment, a ward is renumbered, a new block opens and half the directory shifts. Printed signage has a lead time measured in weeks and a cost measured per board. A digital layer has neither, so the practical rule is that the map is the master record and the printed boards are a derived snapshot, reissued on a fixed cycle rather than on every change.

The map editor is built for this. A facilities administrator moves a POI, renames a room or adds an entrance without touching code, and the change propagates to kiosks, the message link, the QR posters and any embedded app on the next publish. We usually recommend a weekly publish window plus an emergency path for clinical moves, so a relocated clinic does not wait for the next cycle. Every change is logged, which matters when estates is asked why a route changed last Tuesday.

  • Map is the master record, printed boards are a snapshot
  • POI moves, room renames and new entrances need no code change
  • Weekly publish window plus an emergency path for clinical moves
  • Each map change carries a log entry for estates audit

06

Accessibility

How do step-free routes and accessibility work in practice?

Accessibility is a route property, not a separate product. Each edge of the map carries attributes: stairs, escalator, lift, ramp, door width, whether a lift serves that floor, whether the route crosses a threshold. A step-free request then becomes a graph query rather than a hand-curated path, and it stays correct when a lift goes out of service if the operator marks that lift unavailable and the route recalculates around it.

For visitors with low vision the interface supports large type, high contrast and spoken guidance, and the AR view works alongside speech rather than replacing it. For foreign patients the interface runs in any Latin-script language plus Arabic with right-to-left layout, Chinese, Japanese and Korean, with each site's content managed separately so a satellite clinic can carry a different set. The honest trade-off: step-free routes are often longer, so we show the extra walking time instead of hiding it.

  • Route edges carry lift, ramp, stair and door-width attributes
  • Out-of-service lifts can be flagged without remapping the floor
  • Large type, high contrast, spoken guidance and RTL Arabic supported
  • Longer step-free routes display their extra walking time

07

Human factors

Designing for an anxious first-time visitor

The first visit is the hard case. The visitor is anxious, often accompanied, frequently late, and reading a letter rather than a screen. Three choices help more than any feature list. The route starts where the person actually is, not at a main entrance they still have to find. The words match the letter they are holding: building name, department name, floor, room. And the route ends at a landmark a person can recognize, such as a reception desk or a numbered door, rather than at a coordinate on a plan.

We also place 720 degree panorama stills at the two or three junctions where people genuinely get lost, usually where a corridor forks between two blocks. A still taken from the junction with the correct corridor marked resolves more confusion than a longer set of written instructions. Location sharing by link lets a relative send a live position to the person collecting them, which quietly removes a share of the phone calls that reception currently absorbs.

  • Route begins at the visitor's current position, not the front door
  • Destination language matches the appointment letter
  • Panorama stills at the two or three real decision points
  • Live location sharing by link for relatives and escorts

08

Schedule

How long does a hospital deployment take?

A single outpatient building is a short project. A multi-block campus is not, mostly because the floor plans are inconsistent and access to clinical areas is windowed. The reference schedule is typical rather than fixed: survey three to five days for 50,000 to 100,000 square meters, map production two to three weeks, platform setup and directory integration two to four weeks, calibration and training one week. Total six to ten weeks, phased so the first building is live while the second is surveyed.

Phasing matters more in healthcare than in retail. Start with the building that generates the most wayfinding complaints, prove the map maintenance workflow with the estates team who will own it, then extend. A campus that tries to map every block in one pass usually ends with an accurate map that nobody updates after the handover month.

  • Survey window depends on clinical access, not on floor area alone
  • First building live while the second is surveyed
  • Maintenance workflow proven with the team who will own it

FAQ

Common questions

Can't see your question? Send it with your floor count and we answer within one working day.

Ask a specific question

Can hospital wayfinding connect to our appointment system?

We read the department directory on a read-only basis, so clinic codes resolve to the rooms they currently occupy. Messages and letters can carry a route link that opens pre-aimed at the right building and clinic. Nothing is written back and no patient record is queried, which keeps the integration inside a standard data protection review.

Does the system track patients through the building?

No. Counting and heatmaps use aggregate search, route request and sensor data per zone and hour. There is no camera-based identity tracking, no facial recognition and no link between a route and a patient record. Staff location runs only on consenting badges during shifts, for dispatch, portering and emergency response.

What happens when a clinic moves to another floor?

A facilities administrator edits the room or department record in the map editor and republishes. Kiosks, the message link and any embedded app pick up the change on the next publish, which we recommend running weekly with an emergency path for clinical moves. No code change and no new hardware are involved.

Do visitors need to download an app?

No. Routes open from a link or a QR poster in the phone browser, and the same map feeds the lobby kiosks. An app or mini program is optional where a hospital already runs one, and the SDK is available for that case. Most outpatient visits are served by the browser route alone.

How accurate is indoor positioning inside a hospital?

Typical horizontal accuracy is 1 to 3 meters with BLE, 0.1 to 0.3 meters with UWB, 3 to 8 meters with Wi-Fi fingerprinting and 1 to 2 meters with hybrid BLE, Wi-Fi and map matching. The figure is site dependent because thick walls and dense equipment reduce it, so we survey and report measured accuracy in the actual corridors.

Can it route wheelchair users without stairs?

Yes. Step-free is a route profile rather than a separate map. Lifts, ramps, door widths and thresholds are attributes on the map network, so a step-free request avoids stairs and escalators entirely and recalculates when a lift is marked out of service. Longer step-free routes show their extra walking time.

How much floor plan detail do you need from us?

CAD or vector floor plans per level are ideal, plus a room list with current codes and any aliases. Where only PDFs or scans exist, the survey phase covers the gap and takes longer. We return a room list with conflicts flagged, usually the first time the timetabling codes and the space register have been compared.
