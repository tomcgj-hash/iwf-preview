01

Definition

What does white label mean in a wayfinding deployment?

White label means a visitor never sees our name. The app icon, the launch screen, the mini program entry, the web portal, the QR posters at the doors and the printed leaflet all carry the brand of the venue, the operator or the integrator. What sits underneath is one mapping and routing engine, maintained by us, with the venue's map, tenants and content loaded on top of it.

The split is worth being precise about, because white label is often used loosely. We do not hand over the source of the routing engine, and we do not ask you to run a mapping team. You own the brand, the content, the tenant data and the relationship with the venue or the end client. We own the engine, the map tooling and the platform code, and we keep them updated. That division is what makes the arrangement cheap to start and stable to run.

For a property group, the result is one app across a portfolio with a single design language, one tenant admin model and one reporting view, even where each mall keeps its own colors and its own name. For an integrator, white label turns a service they would otherwise sub contract into a product line they can sell and support without building navigation software or hiring a mapping team from nothing.

02

Branding

What carries your brand?

The brandable surface covers every point where a visitor or a tenant meets the product. That means the mobile app on both platforms, the mini program inside the messenger ecosystem the venue uses, the web portal at a domain you own, the kiosk interface skin, the QR entry posters and the printed leaflet. Logo, color palette, typography and icon set are applied per deployment, and one tenant of the platform can hold several brand skins at once, which is how a portfolio keeps each mall distinct.

Practically, branding is a configuration set rather than a code fork. A skin is applied to the app build, the portal theme and the kiosk layout together, so a rebrand does not turn into three separate projects with three delivery dates. Where a venue needs a change to the interface structure rather than the skin, that moves into the SDK work rather than the brand layer, and we scope it separately.

  • Native app builds on iOS and Android with your icon and name
  • Mini program entry under the venue or operator account
  • Portal on a domain you own, with your certificate
  • Kiosk interface skin matched to the venue brand
  • QR posters, leaflets and door labels in the same identity
  • Multiple brand skins in one platform tenant

03

Boundaries

Who owns what after launch?

Two lines in that table matter most in a procurement review. The first is the app store account: if the listing sits under your developer account, the venue cannot be locked out of its own app by a supplier change. The second is the tenant data export. A venue that can leave with its map and its tenant register is a venue that will sign in the first place.

04

Operations

What can the venue team do without us?

The tenant-facing admin is a scoped portal, not the full platform console. A venue administrator adds and edits tenant records, moves a tenant between units, updates opening hours, publishes a directory change and schedules a campaign. They see the screens and the kiosks in the venue with their state, and they can raise a support ticket from the same screen. What they cannot do is reach another venue in the portfolio or change platform settings.

Roles keep that boundary enforceable. A center manager edits tenant data, a marketing coordinator edits campaigns, an operations lead sees terminal health, and each role sees only its own venue. Every change is logged with a user and a timestamp, so a wrong tenant name in a directory can be traced to the edit that introduced it. That audit trail is usually the feature that convinces a landlord to let the tenant team near the data at all.

  • Tenant records, units, categories and opening hours
  • Kiosk and screen state for the venue only
  • Campaign scheduling within an approved template set
  • Role based access per venue and per function
  • Change log with user, time and previous value
  • Support ticket raised into the partner desk

05

Reseller terms

How does reselling work for integrators and signage companies?

An integrator can license the platform, brand it as their own product and sell it into their own client base with their own support desk in front. In that model we sit behind the integrator: we handle second line support, engine updates and map production queries, and the integrator handles the client relationship, first line support, installation and hardware. The client sees one supplier, and that supplier is the integrator.

The commercial structure is a license per deployment with a support term, and volume terms where an integrator commits to a number of venues per year. Because the engine is shared, an integrator does not need to employ a navigation team to carry the product, but they do need one person who can read a floor plan and run a survey, because map production quality decides whether the deployment holds up in year three. We train that person rather than replace them, and we review the first two maps with them line by line before anything goes out under the partner name.

We do not sell directly into a client account that an integrator has brought to us. That is the clause that matters most when a signage company evaluates the arrangement, and it is written into the partner agreement rather than left to good will. In exchange, the integrator commits to keeping the client current on releases and reporting site issues back to us, because a stale deployment behind a partner brand is hard to diagnose after the fact.

  • License per deployment with a defined support term
  • Integrator brand on app, portal, kiosk and documentation
  • First line support with the integrator, second line with us
  • Deal registration so we do not approach your client directly
  • Volume terms for committed annual deployment counts
  • Training for one map production and one support lead per partner

06

Development

What do the SDK options cover?

Three levels cover most partner needs. The integration SDK is the lightest: it embeds a route view or a location share inside an app the partner already owns, through a web view or a native component, with the map and routing served by the platform. The native SDK exposes map control, positioning, route calculation and geofence events to code the partner writes, for a product that needs navigation inside its own interface rather than beside it.

The API layer sits under both. A REST surface covers tenant data, points of interest, route requests, geofence state and reporting exports, and it is what a partner uses to keep a CRM, a parking system or a room booking tool in step with the map. Everything the portal can do, the API can do with a token and the right scope, which is what makes a partner able to build an operations dashboard of their own without asking us for a feature.

  • Integration SDK: embedded route view and location share
  • Native SDK: map, positioning, routing, geofence events
  • REST API: tenants, points of interest, routes, reporting
  • Webhook events for entry, exit and route completion
  • Scoped tokens per role and per venue
  • Sandbox tenant for development and acceptance testing

07

Boundaries

What stays ours, and why does that matter to you?

The engine, the map tooling and the platform code stay with us. The reason is maintenance, not control. Indoor routing depends on continuous work: floor geometry, accessible path rules, cross building handover, positioning calibration across three radio technologies, and the small corrections that only show up when a venue opens a new lift. A partner who forks the code inherits that workstream and usually discovers the cost in year two, when the venue has changed and the fork has not.

What you keep is everything that makes the deployment yours: the brand, the domain, the app listing, the tenant data and the client relationship. If the agreement ends, we export the venue map and tenant register in a documented format and the partner keeps the brand assets and the client. That is a fair split, and stating it plainly at the start removes the argument at renewal.

08

Delivery

How long does a white label launch take?

A partner launch has two parts: the partner platform, which is configured once, and the venue deployments that follow. Partner configuration covers branding, domain, roles, content templates, kiosk skins and the SDK keys, and typically runs 2 to 3 weeks once the brand assets and the domain are ready. It does not depend on any single venue, so the partner can finish the platform while the first survey is still being scheduled, and the first venue then starts from a finished product rather than from a bare tenant.

Each venue then follows the standard path: survey 3 to 5 days for 50,000 to 100,000 m2, map production 2 to 3 weeks, platform setup and integration 2 to 4 weeks, on-site calibration and training 1 week. Phased delivery lands at 6 to 10 weeks per venue, typical and confirmed after survey. A partner who has already launched one venue usually runs the second faster, because the templates, the survey method and the acceptance test record are already in place and the client facing risks were settled once.

FAQ

Common questions

Ask a specific question

Can the app be published under our own developer account?

Yes, and we recommend it. The listing, the screenshots, the privacy policy and the reviews sit under the partner account, so the venue or the partner holds the asset. We provide the build, the metadata pack and the review support notes, and the partner submits. A supplier change then does not cost the venue its app.

How much of the interface can we change?

Colors, typography, icon set, launch screen, splash, language of the interface strings and the layout of the home screen are configurable per deployment. Deeper structural changes, such as a navigation flow built around a booking journey, are SDK work and are scoped separately. The brand layer never requires a code fork.

What happens if we end the agreement?

You keep the brand, the domain, the app listing, the tenant data and the client relationship, and we export the venue map and tenant register in a documented format within an agreed window. The engine license stops. What you cannot do is continue to run our engine, which is why the export format is specified in the agreement rather than negotiated at exit.

Can we host it in our own data center?

Yes. The platform runs as SaaS, in a private cloud or fully on premise, and on premise is common with partners who sell into government buildings or hospital groups with data residency rules. The on premise build needs a Linux host, a database and object storage, and it updates on a release package rather than automatically.

Do we need engineers to run this product?

No engineers are needed to deploy and operate it. A map production lead who can read a floor plan and a support lead who can triage a kiosk fault cover the day to day. Engineers are needed only if the partner builds on the SDK or the API, and even then the work is integration rather than navigation code.

Can one platform tenant hold several venue brands?

Yes. A portfolio tenant holds many venues, each with its own skin, its own map and its own tenant admin scope, while the parent account keeps the reporting view across the set. A partner with several clients runs the same way, with the client as the brand owner and the partner as the operator.

How is support split between us and you?

The partner takes first line: the venue calls the partner, the partner triages, and most issues are content, network or hardware. Second line comes to us for engine behavior, map corrections and platform defects, under the support term in the license. We train the partner's first line team and provide the escalation path with response targets.
