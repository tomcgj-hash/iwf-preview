INDOOR GEOFENCE

Indoor geofence: zones, triggers and alerts inside a building

An indoor geofence is a boundary drawn on a venue floor plan that generates an entry, exit or dwell event when a tracked device crosses it. Venues use fences for restricted areas, staff zones, emergency mustering, exhibition stands and tenant boundaries. Alerts can be a message, a voice call or a trigger count.

01

Definition

What is an indoor geofence?

An indoor geofence is a closed shape drawn on a venue floor plan, with rules attached to it. When a tracked device crosses the shape, the platform records an entry or an exit event. When the device stays inside past a time threshold, it records a dwell event. The shape can follow a room, a corridor, a hall, a loading dock or a single exhibition stand.

The fence itself is not the product. The product is the decision that follows: a message to a supervisor, a voice call to a duty phone, a count added to a dashboard, or an action passed to another system such as access control. A fence with no rule attached is just a drawing.

Fences work from the same positioning layer as navigation and staff tracking, which means the zone set, the floor model and the accuracy figure are shared. That is convenient, and it is also the main risk: a fence drawn on a map that is two meters out of alignment will fire on the wrong side of a wall.

02

Constraints

Why do outdoor fence technologies fail indoors?

Satellite positioning does not resolve inside a building. Signals are attenuated by the roof and reflected by steel and glass, so a fix indoors either fails or lands tens of meters away, often on the wrong floor. A surprising number of procurement documents still assume that satellite positioning continues inside the building. It does not, and the methods below are the alternatives the industry actually uses.

Indoor fences therefore rely on BLE beacons or gateways, UWB anchors, Wi-Fi fingerprinting, or a hybrid of these with map matching. Typical horizontal accuracy is 1 to 3 m with standard BLE, 0.1 to 0.3 m with UWB, 3 to 8 m with Wi-Fi, and 1 to 2 m with a hybrid approach. Floor determination comes from a barometer, map matching or a beacon floor identifier.

The practical consequence is that a fence boundary needs a margin. A fence that runs along a shopfront glass, where people stand on both sides, will produce events from the wrong side of the glass at every accuracy level short of UWB. Fences work best along solid, unambiguous geometry: a wall, a doorway, the edge of a hall.

03

Use cases

Which venue use cases justify a fence?

Five patterns cover most requests. They differ in who receives the alert, how fast the response has to be and what counts as an acceptable false positive rate, not in the drawing work itself. The geometry is usually the easy part. Getting a named owner and a response path agreed before the fence goes live is what decides whether the rule survives its first month.

Restricted zones protect plant rooms, roof access, cash offices and back-of-house corridors. Staff zones separate cleaner and security routes from public circulation, which matters during events and out of hours. Mustering zones are the assembly points used after an evacuation, where a fence counts arrivals so a warden can see whether a zone is filling. Exhibition stand zones belong to the organiser rather than the building, and they change with every event. Tenant boundary fences support delivery rules, shared loading docks and disputes about who occupies a service corridor.

04

Mechanics

How is a fence drawn and triggered?

The zone is drawn in the map editor as a polygon or a set of rooms, on a named floor. Each zone gets a rule set: which device group it applies to, whether entry, exit or dwell is the trigger, the dwell timer, the trigger count before an alert fires, and the notification channel. Rules can be limited to a time window, so a staff zone only applies outside public opening hours, or to a date range, which is how exhibition stand zones are set per event.

Two parameters do most of the tuning. The dwell timer suppresses alerts from people who walk through a doorway without stopping. The trigger count suppresses single device noise: a rule that requires three entry events from three distinct devices within five minutes will not fire on one stray position fix.

Alerts are delivered as a platform message, a push notification to a duty phone, a voice call to a named number, or an entry in a dashboard queue. Voice is used where the alert must interrupt someone who is not looking at a screen, which in practice means security supervisors and plant room duty staff.

  • Zone geometry drawn on the map editor, reusable across events
  • Entry, exit and dwell triggers with per zone timers
  • Trigger counts to filter single device noise
  • Time windows and date ranges per rule
  • Message, push, voice or dashboard delivery
  • Every event logged with zone, time and device group

05

Calibration

Why do fences produce false positives, and how do you fix them?

Most false positives come from three causes: map alignment, boundary geometry and floor confusion. Alignment is a survey problem, and it is fixed by walking the boundary with the calibration tool during commissioning rather than by adjusting rules later. Geometry is a design problem: a fence that runs through open floor instead of along a wall will always be noisy.

Floor confusion is the one that surprises operators. A device on the floor above can resolve to a fence on the floor below when vertical separation is small and the floor is determined by signal strength alone. Adding a beacon floor identifier on each level, or a barometer based floor determination, removes most of it. Where it cannot be removed, a fence rule that requires two consecutive events reduces the noise at the cost of a few seconds of latency.

Tune in this order: survey and alignment, then geometry, then floor determination, then timers, then trigger counts. Changing rules before the map is right produces a system that reports fewer alerts and misses more real events, which is worse than a noisy one.

06

Privacy

What has to be in place before a fence is lawful?

Fences only work on devices that are being tracked, and tracking needs a basis. For visitors that means an opt-in app that explains what the venue records and why. For staff that means issued devices or badges, a stated purpose, a shift-scoped visibility window, and a written retention limit. A fence that tracks staff through personal phones without an agreement is a dispute waiting to happen.

We do not support camera based identity tracking or biometric identification in the fence layer. A fence event records that a device crossed a boundary, not who the person is. Where a venue also operates CCTV, the two systems stay separate and are governed by separate policies.

The workable design is narrow: few fences, each with a named owner, a stated purpose, a defined device group and an automatic expiry date for the rule. Fences that were created for one event and never closed are the most common audit finding we see.

FAQ

Common questions

Can't see your question? Send it with your floor count and we answer within one working day.

Ask a specific question

How small can a geofence be?

Smaller than the positioning accuracy is pointless. With UWB at 0.1 to 0.3 m a single desk area can be fenced with confidence. With standard BLE at 1 to 3 m the practical minimum is roughly a room or a stand of several meters across. Draw the fence to the geometry you can measure, not to the drawing line.

Can a fence alert someone outside the platform?

Yes. Alerts can be delivered by message, push notification or voice call to a named number, and events can be passed to another system through the API. Access control and fire alarm are the two most common destinations, since both already have a duty response path. The alert should name the zone and the rule, not just the fact that something fired.

Do visitors need to install an app?

For visitor facing fences, yes, because a device has to be tracked with consent. Some venues avoid that entirely by fencing only staff devices and asset tags, which covers restricted areas, mustering and equipment movement without touching public data. That is usually the better first phase, since it proves the rules and the response path before any public opt-in is asked for.

What happens if a fence fires on the wrong floor?

That is floor confusion, and it usually means the level is being inferred from signal strength alone. Adding a beacon floor identifier on each level, or barometer based determination, fixes most cases. A rule requiring two consecutive events is the fallback when the vertical separation is too small to resolve.

Can fences be created per event and removed afterwards?

Yes, and they should be. Exhibition stand zones are typically created from a hall layout, published for the show dates, and expired automatically at close. Every dated rule should carry an expiry so a temporary fence cannot quietly become permanent.

Does a fence store a path history?

It can, if the venue enables history for that device group, but a fence does not require it. Most deployments log the crossing event only. Where history is enabled for staff devices, retention is short and playback is role restricted.

How many fences can one venue run?

There is no fixed limit, but there is an operational one. Past a few dozen active rules the alert stream becomes noise and nobody reads it. Group zones into rule sets and keep the number of people who can create a fence small.
