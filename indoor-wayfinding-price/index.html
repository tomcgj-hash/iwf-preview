01

Cost drivers

What drives the cost of an indoor wayfinding system?

Seven variables move the number, and they do not move it equally. Area matters less than floor count, because each additional level adds a transition, a set of junctions and a naming problem. Point of interest volume matters more than either, because every tenant or department is a content task performed once by a person and then maintained. Kiosk count plus the cost of getting power and data to each position is the third, and in a finished concourse the mounting is often larger than the hardware.

Positioning density is the fourth: gateways along walking paths and around junctions, with spacing set by corridor width and ceiling height rather than by a fixed ratio to floor area. Integrations are the fifth, and they are where estimates go wrong, because a venue app or a parking system with a documented interface is a week of work while an undocumented one is a discovery project. Languages are the sixth, since each one adds content and a review pass, and Arabic adds right-to-left layout work. AR is the seventh, priced per junction authored rather than per floor covered.

  • Floor count, more than total floor area
  • POI and tenant volume, a content task per item
  • Kiosk count plus power, data and mounting per position
  • Positioning density by corridor and ceiling, not by ratio
  • Integrations, priced by interface quality
  • Language packs, with RTL layout review for Arabic
  • AR authoring, priced per junction rather than per floor

Ask any supplier to quote these seven lines separately. A single delivered figure hides which variable is driving it.

02

Scopes

Which delivery scope fits your venue?

There are three honest scopes, and the cheapest one is often the right one for the first year. Directory and map only gives you a 3D multi-floor map, directory kiosks and tenant search, with no positioning hardware and no phone navigation. It suits a venue whose visitors mostly need to find a unit from the entrance, and it is the fastest route to something live.

Navigation and positioning adds gateways, phone routing, QR handover and location sharing on top of the same map, so a visitor can be guided from the entrance to a unit and a colleague can be sent a link to a meeting point. Full operations and AR adds parking guidance, reverse car finder, people counting, geofencing, staff dispatch and patrol, AR authoring at selected junctions and the reporting that ties them together.

The important property is that the scopes stack. The map, the tenant data and the platform do not need to be rebuilt between phases, which is why starting narrow is a defensible commercial decision rather than a compromise. What you cannot add later without rework is map quality, so insist on a surveyed, signed-off map even in the smallest scope.

03

Two models

How is a price built per square meter and per floor?

The two models answer different questions, and mixing them without saying so is how comparisons go wrong. A per square meter model prices the map and the positioning as a function of surveyed area. It suits large, open, single-level buildings with a low content density: exhibition halls, warehouses, big-box retail, where the floor is mostly circulation and each additional thousand square meters adds little new content.

A per floor model prices the transitions and the junctions, and it suits buildings where the cost is concentrated in vertical movement and repeated decision points: a hospital tower, a mall with six public levels, a campus with linked blocks. Each level carries a set of junctions, a lift lobby, a naming problem and a calibration walk, and those costs do not scale with its area. A tall narrow building is expensive per square meter and ordinary per floor; a wide low building is the reverse.

The practical answer is usually a hybrid stated openly: map production by area band, content by POI count, hardware by device count, authoring by junction. The worksheet below is the shape of that quote. Quantities are printed without amounts, because the amounts depend on the survey and on shipping and installation conditions that differ by region. State the model in one line at the top of the proposal, so the reader knows which dimension is being bought.

04

Regions

What changes the number between regions?

The hardware bill and the labor bill behave differently. In the Middle East and Gulf, large mixed-use malls and multi-language requirements are the norm, so the content and language lines grow while the kiosk count per square meter stays modest. Summer installation windows and cooling during commissioning also affect scheduling, which in turn affects the on-site days you budget for.

Southeast Asia and Africa usually see the labor share of the budget rise relative to hardware, because installation, calibration and content entry are performed locally while hardware is imported. Import duty, certification and shipping can move the hardware line materially, and the realistic planning assumption is a buffer on landed cost rather than a fixed figure. In both regions phased delivery is common, which suits a map-first scope.

Latin America and Europe shift weight to compliance and integration. Language variants matter in Europe, as do data protection reviews when positioning, counting or cameras are involved; those reviews add calendar time rather than a large line item, but they add time on the critical path. In Latin America, service coverage for maintenance is the usual constraint, which pushes toward fewer devices with a higher standard of installation.

What does not change is the shape of the quote. The lines are the same everywhere; only their proportions and the calendar move. That is why we quote against the worksheet above rather than against a per square meter figure, which would flatter some regions and mislead in others. A single per square meter figure quoted for a six-floor mall assumes an unusual geometry or an unvisited building.

  • Middle East and Gulf: multi-language content, tight summer windows
  • Southeast Asia: labor-import mix, duty and landed cost buffer
  • Africa: import certification, phased delivery, service coverage
  • Latin America: maintenance coverage favours fewer, better devices
  • Europe: data protection review adds calendar time on the critical path

05

Proposal

What do we need from you to issue a budgetary proposal?

Five things are enough for a first budgetary proposal: the number of public floors, the approximate area per floor, the number of tenants or departments that must be searchable, whether parking is in scope, and which languages the interface needs. With those, we can price the structure, name the assumptions and mark which lines will move after the survey. Those five answers fix the shape of the scope; everything else refines it.

A short survey follows, either from your drawings and a video walk of the public routes, or on site for larger venues. The survey fixes the quantities that the estimate could only band: gateway density per corridor, kiosk positions and their power routes, junction count for AR authoring, and the interfaces that need an adapter. We issue the budgetary proposal within 24 hours after that short survey.

Every line in the proposal is written to be checked. Where a figure depends on an assumption, the assumption is printed beside it, so procurement can challenge it rather than accept or reject the whole. If a line is optional, it is marked optional. If a scope choice would make the project cheaper, we say so, including when the honest answer is that the smallest scope is enough for the first year.

  • Public floor count and approximate area per floor
  • Number of searchable tenants or departments
  • Whether parking guidance and reverse finder are in scope
  • Interface languages, including any right-to-left requirement
  • Any existing parking, CRM or signage system to connect

Drawings plus a phone video of the public routes are often enough to skip the site visit at the estimate stage.

06

Phasing

How can you keep the first phase small without wasting it?

Phase the coverage, not the quality. A single fully surveyed floor with a complete tenant list is worth more than five floors with approximate geometry, because the approximate map fails at exactly the junctions visitors ask about and the whole system is judged on that failure. One floor, done properly, is a defensible first phase and produces evidence for the next budget cycle.

Reuse what exists. If the venue already runs a parking system with plate recognition or a signage CMS, integrating it is cheaper than running a parallel stack, and it avoids the two-systems-disagree problem that visitors notice immediately. If the venue has a digital map from an earlier project, its geometry may be salvageable even when the platform is not. Ask for the interface list before the price, then remove the ones you do not need.

Avoid the reverse economy of skipping calibration. An uncalibrated deployment produces off-route behavior, and off-route behavior is read as a broken product rather than as a missing step. Calibration days are one of the cheapest lines on the worksheet and one of the most expensive to omit, since the fix is a return visit with travel and scheduling attached. That return visit is also the line a contractor is most likely to cut to win the job.

  • One floor surveyed properly beats five floors approximated
  • Integrate existing parking or signage rather than duplicating
  • Salvage map geometry from an earlier project where possible
  • Never remove calibration to fit a budget
  • Publish the phase-two trigger before phase one starts

FAQ

Common questions

Ask a specific question

Why is there no list price on this page?

Because the variables move the number by more than any list price could survive. Floor count, POI volume, kiosk positions and power routes, gateway density, interfaces and languages each change the total independently, and shipping and installation conditions differ by region. A published figure would be wrong for almost every reader and would waste the survey that fixes it.

How quickly can we get a budgetary proposal?

Within 24 hours after a short survey. The survey itself needs the public floor count, approximate area per floor, the number of searchable tenants or departments, whether parking is in scope and the interface languages. With drawings and a phone video of the public routes, that can often be done without a site visit at the estimate stage.

Is per square meter or per floor the better model for us?

Per square meter suits wide, open, low-content buildings such as exhibition halls and big-box retail, where more area adds little new content. Per floor suits tall buildings where cost sits in vertical transitions, lift lobbies and repeated junctions. A hospital tower is ordinary per floor and expensive per square meter, and the reverse holds for a warehouse.

What is the smallest useful first phase?

One public floor, fully surveyed, with a complete tenant or department list and the directory live. That produces a map you can extend, real search data and evidence for the next budget cycle. A partial map across many floors costs more and fails at the junctions visitors actually ask about, which damages the case for phase two.

Do we pay for the survey?

The survey is a line on the proposal, not a hidden cost. Where drawings and a route video are sufficient for the estimate, the fieldwork is deferred to the delivery phase and the proposal is issued from those materials. Where the site is large or the garage geometry is unclear, we recommend the visit and say why before it is scheduled.

Are hardware and software priced together?

They are quoted as separate lines even when they arrive as one contract. That separation matters at renewal: hardware carries a 12-month warranty under the standard term, while software updates run during the support term. Splitting the lines lets you compare a proposal against an existing platform, and it makes the multi-year cost visible at the start.

How much do extra languages add?

One line per language, covering interface strings and content review, and Arabic adds right-to-left layout work that is a design task rather than a translation task. The cost does not scale with floor area, so a multi-language venue on a small footprint sees the language share rise. Per-venue content is managed separately, which is what makes regional rollouts practical.

What do integrations add to the budget?

It depends on the interface, not on the system type. A documented REST interface is a short, predictable line. An undocumented one, or one that requires reading a vendor's database, is scoped as discovery first. We price interfaces individually so that the ones you do not need can be removed without renegotiating the whole proposal.

Can we reuse hardware we already own?

Sometimes. Directory terminals of the right specification can be reused if the platform supports them, and existing parking cameras can feed plate events if they expose a readable stream. Reuse usually reduces the hardware line and increases the integration line. Ask for both figures rather than accepting a net change, because the split tells you where the risk sits.
