Museums

Museum wayfinding that follows the curator, not just the shortest corridor

A museum route is a curatorial decision before it is a navigation one. We map galleries as a sequence rather than a grid, publish temporary exhibitions as a dated layer, couple audio tracks to gallery zones, and route timed ticket holders to the right door at the right minute.

01

Route intent

Why is a museum route different from a shortest path?

In most venues the right route is the shortest one. In a museum it is often the intended one. Curators build galleries in a deliberate sequence, with sightlines, thresholds and a narrative order that a shortest-path algorithm will happily demolish by sending visitors through a service stair. The route engine has to accept an authored order as a first-class input, with detours marked optional, so a visitor who wants the highlights gets the highlights in the order the curator set rather than in the order the building allows.

That changes what the map shows. A good museum map displays rooms and a sequence of rooms, not just distances. We model each gallery as a POI with a position in any number of published routes, so one gallery can appear third on a 45 minute highlights tour, first on a family route, and off-route entirely for a visitor heading to one specific object.

  • Authored stop order accepted as a routing input
  • Optional detours marked without breaking the sequence
  • One gallery can sit in several published routes

02

Route set

Which route types does a museum actually need?

Most museums need four or five published routes rather than one. Each is a sequence with an estimated duration, a step-free flag and a set of stops, and each is edited independently when a gallery closes for a changeover. Publishing them as separate routes on one dataset means a closed room is flagged once and every affected route recalculates, instead of an operations team maintaining five versions of the same plan.

Route types and what each one does

03

Exhibition layer

How do temporary exhibitions get published and removed?

A temporary exhibition is a layer with dates. The galleries it occupies are remapped for the run, its stops are added to the relevant routes, its entry door becomes a timed destination, and the whole layer is switched off on the closing date without touching the permanent collection underneath. That matters because changeovers happen on a fixed calendar whether or not anyone has time to redraw a map.

The map editor is the tool for this. A curator or exhibitions coordinator adds stops, renames a room, closes a gallery with a notice, and republishes. No code change and no vendor ticket. We recommend one publish at the start of the run and one at the end, plus an emergency path for a closure caused by a withdrawn loan or a conservation issue.

  • Dated layer switched on and off without remapping
  • Stops, doors and closures edited by museum staff
  • Emergency path for a mid-run gallery closure

04

Audio coupling

How does an audio guide fit with navigation?

Audio guides and wayfinding get bought separately and then fail together, because the visitor ends up holding two things and looking at neither. We couple them at the zone level. A gallery is drawn as an indoor geofence, entry triggers that zone's track on the visitor's phone, and the same zone supplies the next-stop prompt in curatorial order rather than pointing at whichever gallery happens to be nearest.

For museums without a handset fleet the coupling still works. A QR code at the gallery threshold opens the track in the browser, and the route to the next stop is one tap away. The interpretation content stays with the museum. We provide the trigger, the sequence and the route, not the script.

  • Gallery as geofence, entry event fires the track
  • Next-stop prompt follows the sequence, not proximity
  • QR at the threshold for venues without handsets
  • Museum keeps authorship of all interpretation content

05

Timed entry

How do timed tickets and time slots change routing?

Timed entry turns a museum into a scheduling system. A visitor holding a 14:30 slot for a special exhibition needs to be at a specific door by 14:25, and the queue at that door is often the longest wait of the visit. Routing handles it as two linked legs: a route into the permanent collection that respects the slot, and a timed prompt to head for the exhibition door with walking time included, so the visitor is not caught in the wrong wing when the slot opens.

The same logic covers talks, tours and school groups, where a group leader needs a meeting point and a head count rather than an individual route. Where a booking system exists, the slot is passed to the map as an appointment and the route is built backwards from the timed door, so the departure prompt comes from the walking distance rather than from a fixed warning.

  • Slot passed as an appointment, route built back from the door
  • Walking time included in the departure prompt
  • Meeting points and head counts for guided groups
  • Queue position shown at the timed entry door

06

Fabric limits

What are the constraints in a heritage building?

The honest answer is that heritage buildings cost more per square meter to map and constrain the hardware more than the software. You cannot run cable through a listed wall, you cannot surface-mount a box on a painted panel, and you cannot assume a ceiling void exists. Positioning therefore leans on small concealable devices and on hybrid methods: beacons fitted inside existing light fittings or behind joinery, Wi-Fi fingerprinting where an access point estate already exists, and map matching to keep the estimate honest. UWB is specified only where the fabric and the budget allow.

That trade-off belongs in the conversation before the survey, not after it. In a low, thick-walled gallery the achievable accuracy is worse than in an open atrium, and a supplier who promises otherwise is selling a number rather than a result. We survey, report measured accuracy gallery by gallery, and design the interface so a rough position still yields a correct route.

  • No cabling through listed walls or surface-mounted boxes
  • Beacons concealed in fittings and joinery where required
  • Hybrid positioning with map matching as the fallback
  • Measured accuracy reported per gallery after survey

07

Dark galleries

How do low-light galleries affect the interface?

Dark galleries are a constraint on the screen, not on the map. A bright white interface ruins night vision and irritates everyone in the room, so the visitor app ships a dark mode with low luminance surfaces, and spoken guidance becomes the primary channel rather than the fallback. AR overlays have to be checked against the actual gallery lighting, because an overlay that reads well in a test room can disappear on a dark wall or wash out a lit object.

Kiosks need the opposite treatment: readable in a bright atrium, dimmable for an evening event, and positioned where a spotlight will not flare across the panel. In a heritage entrance hall the kiosk is often the only place a screen is acceptable at all, so placement is a conservation decision as much as a signage one.

  • Dark mode with low luminance surfaces for gallery floors
  • Spoken guidance as a primary channel, not a fallback
  • AR overlays validated under the real gallery lighting
  • Dimmable kiosks for evening events and bright atriums

08

Visitor data

What do visitor flow and heatmaps tell curators?

Aggregate behavior answers curatorial questions a visitor book cannot reach. Which gallery do people search for first, which stops collect the most route requests, where do routes get abandoned, which objects are searched and never found, how long does a highlights route take against its stated duration. The dashboards report popular destinations, active POIs and visitor origin at zone and hour level.

The privacy limit is stated once and enforced. Counts and aggregate patterns, no individual visitor identification, no camera-based identity tracking, no facial recognition, and no link between a route and a ticket holder. A museum can act on where people go without ever knowing who they are, and that distinction is worth stating in the tender response as well as on the wall panel.

  • Searches with no result reveal gaps in the map or the collection
  • Route abandonment locates the confusing junction
  • Popular destinations and active POIs by zone and hour
  • Aggregate only, no identification and no ticket linking

FAQ

Common questions

Ask a specific question

Can museum wayfinding follow a curator's route order?

Yes, and that is the main design decision. The route engine accepts an authored stop order as an input, so a highlights tour runs in the sequence the curator set rather than the shortest path. Detours are optional branches off the sequence. A gallery can sit in several routes at different positions, and closing it updates all of them.

How do temporary exhibitions get added and removed?

As a dated layer. The galleries the exhibition occupies are remapped for the run, its stops are added to the relevant routes, and the layer is switched off on the closing date without touching the permanent collection. Museum staff edit stops, doors and closures in the map editor, so a changeover does not depend on a vendor schedule.

Will it work in a listed building where nothing can be mounted?

Yes, with honest limits. Beacons can sit inside existing fittings or behind joinery, and Wi-Fi fingerprinting uses an access point estate that may already exist, with map matching keeping the estimate reasonable. Accuracy in a thick-walled, low gallery is worse than in an atrium, so we survey and report measured results per gallery rather than quoting a single figure.

Can it trigger audio guide tracks by location?

Yes. A gallery is drawn as an indoor geofence, and entry fires that zone's track on the visitor's phone, with the next stop prompted in sequence rather than by proximity. Where there is no handset fleet, a QR at the gallery threshold opens the track in the browser. The museum keeps authorship of all interpretation content.

How does it handle timed entry tickets?

A time slot is treated as an appointment. The map knows which door the slot admits to, builds a route backwards from that door, and prompts the visitor to leave with the walking time already included. Where a booking system exists the slot is passed in directly. We do not promise admission timing, because queue management stays with the museum.

What does it tell us about visitor flow?

Aggregate patterns at gallery and hour level: which destinations are searched first, which stops collect the most route requests, where routes are abandoned, and which objects are searched without a result. The reports support curatorial and operational decisions. They never identify an individual visitor and never link a route to a ticket.

Do visitors need to download an app?

No. The route opens in the phone browser from a QR code at the entrance or in the gallery, and the same map runs on kiosks in the entrance hall. A museum app or mini program is optional where one already exists, and the SDK is available for it. Most visitors will use the browser route and the audio trigger together.
