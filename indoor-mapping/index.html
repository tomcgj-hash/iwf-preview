INDOOR MAPPING

Indoor mapping: floor plates, walk graph and the data you keep

An indoor map is a layered data set, not a drawing. It holds floor plates, rooms, corridors, doors, points of interest and parking bays, plus a walk graph that records what connects to what. Routing, search, AR guidance and geofencing all read from that same set, which is why the graph decides whether the map works.

01

Anatomy

What an indoor map is made of

An indoor map is a set of layers that share one coordinate frame. The floor plate records the outline of each level at its surveyed height. Rooms and units record enclosed spaces and where their doors are. Corridors and doors record circulation. Points of interest record the places a visitor searches for. The walk graph records what can connect to what. Zones record areas that trigger behavior rather than being drawn for a visitor to look at.

The picture is the least important layer for routing and the most visible to the visitor, which is why weak projects over-invest in the drawing and under-invest in the graph. A handsome plan with a broken graph produces routes that stop at a wall, and no amount of visual polish fixes it. The reverse also holds: a plain plan over a sound graph guides people correctly, and most visitors never look at the drawing closely enough to mind.

Every layer has an owner and an update rhythm. Floor plates and walls change on a construction cycle. Tenants and POIs change monthly. Opening hours and promotion content change weekly. We build the map so the fast-changing layers can be edited by venue staff while the slow ones stay under change control, which keeps a marketing change from needing a survey and a survey from being blocked by a marketing change.

02

How are floors stacked into a 3D map?

Each floor is extruded from its plate to the measured floor-to-floor height and stacked at the true elevation, so the model reads as the building rather than as a pile of drawings. Lift shafts, stair wells and atriums are modelled as continuous volumes across levels, which is what makes a vertical route legible when a visitor rotates the view. A mezzanine that sits half a level up is modelled at its true offset rather than snapped to a whole floor, because the snapping is what produces a route nobody can walk.

The 3D model is for orientation, not for measurement. A visitor uses it to understand that the destination is one level up and across the atrium; the arithmetic of the route still comes from the graph. We keep the geometry light enough to open on a mid-range phone, and we cap detail at what the camera can actually resolve at corridor scale.

03

How do you model the site outside the doors?

Most confusion starts before the visitor is inside, so the site gets its own geometry: the building footprints, the entrances, the driveways and drop-off, the car park ramps and levels, the walkways between buildings, and the pavements that lead to each door. Each entrance is then registered as an anchor that the indoor graph connects to, which is what allows one route to span the street and the fourth floor.

Outdoor and indoor share a single documented reference point, so the two parts of the model cannot drift apart when either is revised. On a campus we model the shared podium or plaza once and attach several buildings to it, rather than repeating the site for each structure. That single site also gives the search a starting point before the visitor is inside, which is the half of the journey most wayfinding systems quietly ignore.

04

How do venue staff change a tenant without a developer?

The map editor runs in the browser. A venue editor adds a point of interest, drops it on the correct unit, fills in the name, category, keywords, opening hours, unit number and languages, and publishes. Retiring a tenant is the reverse: unpublish the POI, keep the unit geometry, and the search index updates on the next publish. The same form handles an event space that changes name weekly and a department that changed once in ten years, which is why most fields are optional rather than mandatory.

Changes go through draft and publish rather than editing live, so a mistake can be reviewed before visitors see it. Every publish creates a revision that can be compared and rolled back, and the audit log records who changed what. Roles are separated: an editor can change content, an admin can change floors and graph, and a viewer sees the published map only.

  • Add, move or retire a POI from a form, no code and no redeploy
  • Draft, preview and publish with a revision history
  • Bulk load a unit list instead of typing hundreds of records
  • Role separation between content editing and structural change
  • Multilingual names edited per venue, per language

05

What data goes in and what comes out?

Inputs are usually the drawings the venue already owns: DWG or DXF from the architect, IFC where a building information model exists, PDF floor plans where nothing else does. We do not trust a drawing blindly. Levels are checked against a site walk, because a 2014 plan with a 2019 fit-out drawn on a napkin is a common starting point, and a map built on the old geometry will route visitors through walls.

Outputs are two files with different jobs. The JSON export carries layers, POIs, the walk graph, attributes and translations, and it is what the routing engine and the search index read. The SVG export carries the floor rendering in a vector format a signage system or a web page can draw. Both are documented and delivered at handover, so the data is not trapped in one viewer.

06

Who owns the map data after handover?

The venue owns the map data produced for it. We deliver the JSON export, the SVG rendering and the editable source, and we keep a copy only for support purposes under the service agreement. Drawings you supplied stay yours, and we do not resell or reuse one venue geometry for another. The clause matters at the end of a contract as much as at the start, so it is written into the agreement rather than settled in a conversation.

The practical test of ownership is whether you could leave. If the only way to read your own POIs is to open someone else product, you do not own the data in any useful sense. Our export is documented field by field, and the content can be read with a text editor, which is why we state it plainly rather than calling it an open standard.

07

How long does production take and what do you need from us?

Production takes 2 to 3 weeks for a reference venue after the survey, and 6 to 10 weeks is the total schedule once platform setup, integration and calibration are included. The long poles are not drawing time. They are the unit list, the opening hours, the entrance list and the decision about which languages the signage must match. A venue that sends a clean unit list on day one saves about a week, and we say so at kickoff rather than at the deadline.

What we need from you: current floor plans per level, the tenant or department list with unit numbers, entrance and lift locations, opening hours, any closed or staff-only areas, and the signage naming you already use. Where those lists do not exist, we capture them on site, and we flag that as survey days rather than absorbing it silently into production.

08

What makes a map routable rather than just a picture?

A graph is routable when every destination is reachable from every entrance, and that is a test we run rather than an intention. The routing-ready check reports isolated islands, POIs with no connecting edge, doors that open onto nothing, and floor changes that exist in the geometry but not in the graph. Those faults are cheap to fix in production and expensive to fix after calibration.

The graph also carries the rules a picture cannot: door type, door width, one-way corridors, staff-only zones, shutter hours, step counts per flight and the direction an escalator runs. Without them, a route can be geometrically correct and practically impossible, which is exactly the failure visitors report as the system being wrong. A fire door that needs a key card should never appear on a visitor route, and the graph is the only layer that can express that.

  • Every POI reachable from every mapped entrance
  • Isolated areas and dead ends listed and resolved before calibration
  • Door type, width and one-way rules stored on each edge
  • Floor changes validated on site, not taken from the drawing
  • Signage naming cross-checked against POI names

FAQ

Common questions

Ask a specific question

Can you build an indoor map without drawings?

Yes. We measure the building on site and produce geometry from the walk, which adds survey days but removes the dependency on drawings that are often out of date. For older assets, a measured survey is usually faster than arguing about which revision is correct, because the building is the only authority that matters to a visitor.

How detailed does the map need to be?

Detailed enough to route and to search, not more. Corridors, doors, unit outlines, vertical connections and POI attributes carry the work. Furniture, ceiling detail and materials add file size and production time without helping a visitor. We add detail where the camera overlay needs a recognisable landmark and leave the rest schematic.

Who updates the map when a tenant moves out?

Venue staff do, in the browser editor. Unpublishing a POI removes it from search and the directory on the next publish, while the unit geometry stays for the next occupant. Structural changes such as a new corridor or a relocated lift need a short remap, which we scope by floor so the cost matches what changed.

Is the map a 2D drawing or a 3D model?

Both, from one data set. The 2D plan is used for step-by-step routes and kiosk display, and the 3D view is used for orientation when a visitor needs to understand levels and voids. Routing always runs on the graph, never on the rendered geometry, so switching views cannot change the route.

Can the map show several languages on one kiosk?

Yes. POI names, categories and labels are held per language, and the kiosk interface switches between them without duplicating the geometry underneath. Arabic is supported with right-to-left layout. We usually plan three to five languages per venue, chosen from the actual visitor mix rather than from a template.

What happens if the map geometry is wrong after go-live?

It is corrected in the editor as a content change, and the fix publishes without a rebuild. Geometry errors that break routing are treated as defects under the acceptance list, which is why the walk test happens against named routes before handover rather than after the first complaint from a visitor.

Do we need a building information model to start?

No, and an IFC model is not a shortcut either. A building information model often carries spaces but not the door widths, shutter hours or step counts a route needs, and it is frequently heavier than the final map. We take what is usable from it and survey the rest rather than assuming the model is authoritative.

How large a venue can one indoor map cover?

Up to 20 levels per building, and several buildings linked on one site. Beyond that the work is administrative rather than geometric: the platform handles multi-venue content with separate editors and publishing per venue, so a large estate is organized as several maps sharing one coordinate convention.
