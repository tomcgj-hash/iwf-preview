INDOOR POSITIONING

Indoor positioning system: choosing between BLE, UWB, Wi-Fi and hybrid

An indoor positioning system estimates where a phone, badge or tag is inside a building and returns a coordinate, a floor and a confidence value. It can run on BLE beacons at 1-3 m, UWB at 0.1-0.3 m, existing Wi-Fi at 3-8 m, or a hybrid that fuses radio with map matching at 1-2 m.

01

Definition

What an indoor positioning system actually measures

An indoor positioning system estimates the position of a handset, a badge or a tag inside a building and expresses it on a floor. The useful output is not one x and y value. It is a triple: horizontal position, floor, and a confidence figure or radius that tells the software how much to trust the estimate before it draws a marker and issues a turn instruction.

Indoor positioning is not satellite positioning under a different name. The phrase indoor GPS appears in many briefs and describes nothing that works inside a steel and concrete structure, because the signal that serves pedestrians outdoors does not survive the roof. What a venue actually buys is a radio or sensor layer on ceilings and walls, a survey that records where that layer sits in the building coordinates, and a calculation that turns signals into a position.

Two modes are common and they are often confused. Device-based positioning locates the visitor's own phone through the venue app or a web page. Asset and staff positioning locates a tag or badge the venue issues and controls. The infrastructure can be shared, the privacy model cannot, so we specify them as separate work packages with separate data rules. The distinction also decides how the work is accepted: a visitor build is measured on route completion and search success, a staff build on dispatch response and patrol compliance.

02

BLE, UWB, Wi-Fi, hybrid

Which positioning options are available?

We specify and integrate four options. All four are mature radio technologies supplied by hardware makers; our work is the survey, the placement plan, the calibration and the software that consumes the position. The accuracy ranges below are planning figures, not guarantees, because metal, water, crowds and ceiling height move the number in every building. None of the four is a product we manufacture, and we select between them against the survey, the acceptance criteria and the budget you are working with.

The table is the short version. The longer version is that the cheapest option is often the correct one, and we say so when the use case allows it rather than quoting the most precise hardware in the catalog. Accuracy you cannot use is a cost with no return, and a proposal that leads with the tightest available figure usually says more about the supplier than about the building.

03

Floor detection

How accurate is positioning across multiple floors?

Horizontal accuracy is the number in every datasheet and the smaller half of the problem. In a building, the floor is the question visitors ask first, and radio alone answers it badly, because a ceiling slab attenuates a signal without ending it. A visitor on level 2 can hear beacons mounted on level 3, and a marker that jumps a floor destroys trust faster than a marker that is three meters out.

We combine three signals for the floor. A barometric sensor in the handset detects the pressure change of roughly one floor and gives the direction of travel, which is reliable during a lift ride or a stair climb. Map matching restricts the estimate to places the visitor could physically be, so a marker cannot appear inside a wall or on a floor with no connection. A beacon floor identifier resolves the tie at the edge of coverage, where two floors overlap.

For the platform, the practical target is a stable floor at all times and a horizontal error inside 2 m at the seventieth percentile. Both are tested in the acceptance walk, on the routes visitors actually use, rather than on a clean path through an empty corridor. We also record the moment of each floor change, because a switch that happens two steps after the landing sends the visitor into the wrong corridor with a correct plan drawn underneath.

04

Can you use the Wi-Fi network we already have?

Often yes, and it is the option we examine first when a venue already runs a dense corporate or guest Wi-Fi estate. Fingerprinting records the signal signature at many points per floor and matches a new reading against that database. Round trip time measurement, where the access points support it, gives a range directly and needs less survey work per point.

The limits are real. A network designed for coverage rather than position has gaps in stairwells, service corridors, plant rooms and lift lobbies, which are exactly the places where a visitor is lost. Changes to access point placement shift the fingerprints, so any Wi-Fi refresh needs recalibration. And 3 to 8 m is fine for a floor-level marker or a heatmap while it is not enough to resolve two adjacent bays.

The decision rule we apply: if the venue already has the network and the requirement is coarse, use it and spend the budget on the map. If the requirement is a stable marker on a phone in a busy mall, plan beacons rather than hoping the Wi-Fi will improve. A mixed approach, beacons in the concourse and Wi-Fi elsewhere, usually costs less than covering every square meter with new hardware.

05

What does a positioning survey involve?

The survey has three parts. A drawing review fixes the coordinate frame and the floor heights from the plans you hold, because the position layer has to share a frame with the map or the marker will drift by the difference. A radio survey then walks the site with a test kit and records signal strength, multipath and coverage holes in the places that matter, which are corridors, lobbies, stairwells and lift landings rather than the middle of open floors.

Placement planning fixes where each device goes, with spacing that follows ceiling height and material. Calibration walks reference points through the floor, records them against surveyed coordinates, and tunes the model until the error is inside the target on the acceptance routes. The sequence is 3 to 5 days of site time for 50,000 to 100,000 m2, and it is repeated only when the building or the radio environment changes.

06

What device data does the positioning layer keep?

The positioning layer handles a device identifier, a floor, a coordinate, a timestamp and a confidence value. It does not need a name, a phone number, a photograph or a payment record, and we do not ask for them. On the visitor side the identifier is anonymous and rotates, so a session can be counted and analyzed without being tied to a person across visits.

Staff positioning is a different arrangement and it is deliberate. It covers consenting staff devices or badges during a shift, for dispatch, patrol check-in and emergency response. It is not camera-based identity tracking and it does not use biometric identification. Retention, access rights and the audit of who queried which track are agreed with your HR and legal owners before the patrol module is switched on, and on-premise deployment is available where the data cannot leave the building.

  • Anonymous, rotating device identifier with no personal data attached
  • No camera-based identification and no biometric matching anywhere in the stack
  • Staff tracking only on consenting devices and badges, during shifts
  • Retention and access configured per venue before go-live
  • On-premise option for data that must stay inside your network

07

Which option fits which building?

A regional mall usually needs a stable marker at 1 to 2 m, so a visitor walking a concourse sees the icon move with them, and BLE with map matching reaches that at a modest device count. A hospital that must resolve which bay a pump is in needs a much tighter figure, and that asset requirement, not the wayfinding, is what justifies the cost of UWB anchors.

An office campus with an existing Wi-Fi estate and a staff app that shows a floor and a zone rarely needs better than 5 m, and adding beacons to it spends money on precision nobody asked for. A warehouse with steel racking is the difficult case: metal scatters radio and the building itself moves, so we plan a denser survey and a shorter recalibration cycle, and we put that in the proposal rather than discovering it during the walk test.

08

What accuracy should we write into the contract?

Write the method before the number. A useful clause names the floors, the routes, the start and end points, the sample size and the percentile, for example a horizontal error under 2 m at the seventieth percentile across eight named routes, tested three times each, in normal operating conditions. A single average figure across a whole building hides the corridors that fail.

Also define what counts as a floor error and how it is scored, because a session that jumps one floor is worse for the visitor than a session with a larger horizontal error. And name the recalibration triggers: a major fit-out, a change of access point model, a new corridor. A contract that fixes accuracy without a recalibration clause is usually renegotiated after the first tenant change.

09

How do positioning, mapping and navigation fit together?

The map supplies the frame: floors, coordinates, walkable edges and destinations. The positioning layer supplies a position and a floor with a confidence value. The navigation engine consumes both and returns steps. Each can be procured and tested separately, which is why we describe them as three layers with three test plans rather than one product with three names. A venue that already owns a map can add a position layer, and a venue with a position layer can replace its map without losing the calibration.

That separation pays off at handover. If you change map content, calibration survives as long as the coordinate frame does. If you change positioning technology, the map and the routing survive untouched. Where the frame itself changes, for instance when a survey is redone to a different datum, the map and the calibration are redone together and we plan them as one work package.

FAQ

Common questions

Ask a specific question

What accuracy can an indoor positioning system realistically reach?

In a finished building, plan on 1-3 m with BLE, 0.1-0.3 m with UWB in line of sight, 3-8 m with an existing Wi-Fi network, and 1-2 m with a hybrid layer. These are typical horizontal figures from survey work, not guarantees. Heavy metal, water features, dense crowds and ceiling height move them, and the site survey exists to tell you which way they move.

Is UWB always better than BLE?

It is more precise, not better. UWB needs anchors at surveyed positions with clear sight to the tag, plus cabling, and it costs more per square meter. It earns that cost when you must resolve a single bay, a machine or a surgical asset. For guiding a visitor down a concourse, BLE at 1-3 m with map matching is enough and cheaper to maintain.

Can we start with Wi-Fi and add beacons later?

Yes, and it is a sensible sequence when a campus already has dense Wi-Fi. Keep the coordinate frame and the map stable, and beacons can be added floor by floor into the same frame without remapping. The calibration for each floor is redone when devices are added to it, so plan the deployment in floor groups rather than as a single cutover.

How often does positioning need recalibrating?

Calibration holds until the radio environment or the geometry changes. In practice that means a large fit-out, new partitions, a change of access point model, a new corridor, or a redone survey datum. We recommend an annual verification walk and a fresh calibration after any of those events, which is cheaper than discovering drift from visitor complaints.

Does positioning work without the visitor phone?

Yes, for staff and assets. Badges and tags report their own position through the same infrastructure, and the platform shows them on the 2D and 3D map for dispatch, patrol and equipment search. Visitor navigation, by contrast, needs a handset or a kiosk, because the visitor has to see the step list somewhere.

What happens to accuracy in a crowded mall?

Crowds absorb radio energy and block line of sight, so both BLE and UWB readings degrade at peak hours. We survey at a normal occupancy and plan spacing for the busy case, add map matching so the estimate follows corridors rather than noise, and accept a wider error during peak rather than promising a figure the building cannot hold.

Is indoor positioning the same as indoor GPS?

No. Indoor GPS is a common phrase with no technical meaning indoors, because the satellite signal is not usable inside a building. Indoor positioning uses radio devices installed in the building, surveyed to its coordinates. If a supplier uses the phrase without explaining the infrastructure, ask what hardware they intend to install and where.

What drives the cost of an indoor positioning project?

Area, floor count, ceiling complexity, device quantity, cabling and power availability, the accuracy target, and how much integration the position feed needs. A single-building venue with phone-only guidance is the smallest scope. We return a quantity schedule with a budgetary proposal within 24 hours of receiving floor plans, before any commitment.

Can positioning data stay inside our own network?

Yes. Private cloud and on-premise deployments keep position data on infrastructure you control, with the same application features. On-premise needs a server or virtual machine, a database, and network access from the devices. For sites bound by internal data rules, this is usually the deciding factor in the deployment model.
