Screen network

Digital directory: screens that orient a visitor before they ask

A digital directory is the screen layer of wayfinding: non-touch directory boards, interactive kiosks and video walls that show floor lists, tenant boards, event boards and promotions. It runs from a signage CMS, can carry paid advertising, and shares one map and tenant database with the wayfinding platform.

01

Definition

What is a digital directory?

A digital directory is the set of screens a venue uses to tell people where things are. It covers the non-touch board that lists floors and tenants at a lift lobby, the touch kiosk that searches and routes, the digital poster that promotes an event, and the video wall that anchors an atrium. In a wayfinding program these screens are not decoration. They are the layer that answers the orientation question before a visitor reaches a member of staff.

What separates a directory from general signage is the data behind it. A directory board is generated from the same floor plan and tenant register as the map inside the navigation platform, so a tenant move updates the board, the kiosk and the app route from one edit. That single source matters more than any individual panel, because a screen that disagrees with the app destroys trust faster than a screen that is simply absent.

02

Selection

Directory board, kiosk or video wall: which do you need?

Start from the question the screen has to answer and the distance the visitor stands from it. A directory board at a lift lobby is a two meter read: the visitor wants the floor list and a tenant name, and touch adds nothing if the board is above a corridor with no standing space. A kiosk earns its cost where the visitor needs a route, not just a list. A video wall is a landmark and an advertising surface first, a directory second, and it only works when the content is designed for viewing from eight meters and more.

Most venues end up with a mix, and the mix should be deliberate. A common pattern is a landscape directory board at the entrance, a kiosk at the main lift lobby, posters on corridor columns, and one video wall facing the atrium. Each screen type carries a different content job, and putting a search interface on a board that nobody can touch is a wasted panel.

03

Content

What content runs on a digital directory?

Five content types cover most of what a directory shows. The floor directory is the static backbone: floor, category, tenant name, unit number. The tenant board lists brands by category on one screen for visitors who browse rather than search. The event board carries what is happening this week, with a date range and a location. Brand promotion runs between wayfinding states and is the part advertisers pay for. The lock screen or idle screen holds the venue identity when nothing else is scheduled.

Good directory content is short. A floor directory that lists 180 tenants on one screen at two meters is unreadable, so the layout separates the categories, paginates the list and gives each tenant a single line. Numbers, floor codes and unit numbers do the work that adjectives would otherwise do. Where the venue has a tenant with a long legal name and a short trading name, the board shows the trading name, because that is the name the visitor is looking for.

  • Floor directory with categories, unit numbers and hours
  • Tenant board sorted by category, alphabetical or footfall
  • Event board with date range, location and time
  • Brand promotion and sponsor slots in the idle loop
  • Lock screen and venue identity between campaigns
  • Emergency overlay for evacuation and closure notices

04

Platform

How does the signage CMS connect to wayfinding?

The CMS holds layouts, playlists, schedules and screen groups. The wayfinding platform holds the map, the tenant register and the route logic. We connect them at the data layer: the CMS reads the tenant register and the floor plan, so a layout is filled from data rather than from a designer keying names into a template. A tenant rename then flows to the board without a graphics job, and a new floor appears in the directory the day it is drawn.

Scheduling runs on the same timeline as the rest of the venue program. A screen group can hold a base playlist for opening hours and a weekend variant, with campaign takeovers that pre-empt the base for a defined window. Emergency messages outrank everything and can be triggered from the operations desk. Each screen reports its own state back, so a black panel in a corridor shows up as an alert rather than as a complaint from a visitor.

  • Layouts filled from the tenant register, not typed by hand
  • Screen groups by venue, floor, zone or screen type
  • Schedule by date, time band, weekday and campaign
  • Emergency overlay with the highest priority
  • Proof of play reporting for advertisers
  • Health monitoring with remote reboot per screen

05

Readability

How do you size text for a screen nobody stands close to?

The working rule is a cap height of about one millimeter for every 300 millimeters of viewing distance for comfortable reading, and a stricter figure for visitors with reduced vision or for a screen read while walking. At three meters a tenant name needs roughly 26 to 30 millimeters of cap height, which on a 55 inch panel means a short list and large type. Signage that crams sixty names onto one board is legible in a photograph and useless in a corridor.

Contrast does more for legibility than size. Dark text on a light ground or light text on a dark ground both work, but mid tone on mid tone does not, and a pale brand color on white fails at any distance. We test with a contrast ratio of at least 4.5 to 1 for body text and 3 to 1 for large text, and we check the panel in its real lighting, including the glare from a glass facade at midday. Motion is a separate cost: scrolling lists and long transitions cost reading time, so the board holds a static frame long enough to be read twice.

  • Around one millimeter of cap height per 300 millimeters of distance
  • Contrast ratio at least 4.5 to 1 for body text
  • Static frames held long enough to read twice
  • Left aligned lists, consistent column order on every screen
  • Floor color coding repeated on every screen in the set
  • Accessibility variant with larger type and no motion

06

Revenue

Can the screen network pay for itself?

Yes, in venues with enough footfall and a landlord willing to sell the inventory. The model is simple: the loop has a fixed number of slots per hour, a share is reserved for wayfinding and venue messages, and the remainder is sold to tenants and to brands outside the center. A directory in a lift lobby has a captive audience for the thirty seconds a visitor waits for a lift, which is the most valuable moment on the network and the easiest to sell.

What makes the model work is measurement. The CMS reports proof of play per screen, so an advertiser sees how many times a slot ran on which screen and in which week. Add people counting from the same platform and the venue can show footfall against screen position, which is the difference between selling a poster and selling an audience. The directory must stay honest while it sells: a promotion that blocks a search for the toilets will be switched off by the operations team within a week.

  • Fixed slot count per hour with a reserved wayfinding share
  • Tenant promotion sold by the landlord or the center team
  • Proof of play per screen, per week, per campaign
  • Footfall by screen position from the people counting module
  • Advertiser approval workflow before a slot goes live
  • Kill switch on any screen for an emergency overlay

07

Specification

What should a directory screen specification contain?

Write the specification around the room, not around the catalog. A 500 nit panel in a dark corridor is waste; a 400 nit panel behind a sunlit atrium is a refund. Portrait orientation suits a floor list, landscape suits a map and a board together, and a video wall needs a controller that takes one signal and splits it across the tiles. The specification should also state the service access, because a screen that cannot be reached without scaffolding will stay broken.

Running hours matter as much as picture quality. A commercial panel rated for continuous duty is the baseline for a screen that runs twelve hours a day, and the enclosure needs ventilation matched to the ambient temperature in summer, not the temperature on the commissioning day. We write the specification with the maintenance interval, the spare panel count and the cable route included, so the tender compares like with like.

  • Brightness matched to measured light at the mounting position
  • Continuous duty rating for screens running shop hours
  • Controller and tiling plan for any video wall
  • Service access from the front or from a reachable rear void
  • Ventilation sized for the hottest week, not the survey day
  • Spare panel count and swap procedure in the handover pack

08

Trade-offs

When does a printed sign still win?

Print wins on short life and on small count. A corridor with four door numbers, a temporary hoarding, a fire evacuation plan that must be displayed by regulation, a wayfinding strip taped to a floor during a refit: all of these are cheaper, faster and more reliable as print. A printed sign has no power, no network, no license and no firmware, and it never shows a blank screen at nine in the morning.

Print also wins where the content is stable and the audience passes at speed. A floor number above a lift door does not need a screen. The honest split is that print carries the fixed facts and the regulatory signs, screens carry anything that changes, anything that needs a route, and anything the venue wants to sell. A venue that installs screens where print would do usually ends up with a network that is expensive to run and hard to keep honest.

FAQ

Common questions

Can't see your question? Send it with your floor count and we answer within one working day.

Ask a specific question

Should the directory screen be touch or not?

Touch when the visitor needs a route or a search. A plain board when the visitor only needs a floor list from two meters. Touch adds cost, cleaning and a failure point, and it needs standing space in front of the panel, which many lift lobbies do not have. The screen type follows the question the visitor arrives with.

How large is readable text on a 55 inch screen at four meters?

At four meters, a comfortable cap height is around 34 to 40 millimeters, which on a 55 inch panel means roughly twelve to sixteen lines of tenant names with a header. Anything denser needs a search or pagination. We size the layout from the mounting height and the approach distance in the survey rather than from the panel diagonal.

Can the directory boards run without the navigation platform?

They can run as a standalone signage network with a manually maintained tenant list, but then the board and the app will drift apart. Most venues run the board from the same tenant register as the map, so one edit reaches every screen, the kiosk and the phone. The CMS works either way; the shared data layer is the part that saves work.

How many advertising slots can a loop carry?

A ten minute loop with sixty second slots holds ten, and the practical share for revenue is four to six once wayfinding, venue messages and an emergency reserve are set aside. Heavier ad loads raise revenue in the short term and push visitors to ignore the screen, which costs the venue the wayfinding job the screen was installed to do.

Do the screens need their own network?

They can share the venue network with a separate virtual network for screens, or run on a dedicated link. Screens pull content and report playback, so the traffic is steady but small. The important control is that no screen should accept inbound connections from outside the venue network, and every player should reach only its CMS endpoint.

How is content kept current after handover?

The venue's marketing or operations team publishes from the CMS with a template and an approval step, and the tenant register is maintained in the map editor. We run a content review in the first weeks and hand over a publishing guide with the layout rules, the naming conventions and the schedule patterns for opening hours, weekends and campaign periods.

What happens to the screens during an evacuation?

An emergency overlay outranks every playlist and can be triggered from the operations desk or from the fire alarm interface where the venue provides one. The overlay replaces the loop on every screen in the affected group with the evacuation route and the exit names. Directory content resumes when the overlay is released.

Is a video wall worth it for wayfinding?

As a wayfinding tool, rarely. As a landmark that helps a visitor orient and as an advertising surface facing the atrium, often yes. The honest test is whether the wall can be read from the distance people actually stand, and whether the venue has content to run for the hours it is on. A wall showing a brand film on a loop is a landmark, not a directory.
