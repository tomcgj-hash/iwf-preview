STAFF POSITIONING

Staff positioning: dispatch, patrol and response, with limits built in

Staff positioning shows where consenting staff devices or badges are during a shift, so a supervisor can dispatch the nearest officer, confirm a patrol checkpoint and account for people after an evacuation. It is not camera based identity tracking and it does not use biometrics. Visibility is shift scoped and history is retained for a fixed, stated period.

01

Scope

What is staff positioning, and what is it not?

Staff positioning is an operations tool. It answers three questions during a shift: who is on the floor, where is the nearest person who can respond, and did the patrol route get walked. That is the whole scope, and it is worth stating that plainly because the same technology can be described as workforce monitoring by someone who wants a different system.

It is not camera based identity tracking. We do not identify people from video and we do not offer face recognition or gait analysis. It is not biometrics: no fingerprint, no voice print, no behavioural profiling. It is not a productivity score for individual staff, and if someone asks for one, the honest answer is that this system will not produce it well.

What it is: the live position of devices that staff carry with consent, on the venue map, with a staff card showing role, team and current task. The device can be a badge, a handset running the venue app, or a terminal at a fixed point. The quality of the data depends on that choice, which is the first design decision in any deployment.

02

Dispatch

How does dispatch work in practice?

A call comes in, usually from a member of the public at a directory kiosk or through the venue app, and lands in the operations queue with a location. The supervisor sees the nearest three officers with their current position, role and last task, and assigns. The assignment appears on the officer's handset with a route drawn on the same map that visitors use.

The gain is in the last hundred meters. A radio call describing a location takes time to interpret, especially in a large center with similar corridors. A dispatch with a drawn route removes the interpretation step, and the acknowledgement timestamp tells the supervisor that someone is actually moving.

Dispatch also closes the loop. When the officer arrives, the assignment records the arrival, and the event stays attached to the case for the incident log. That record is what makes the tool useful in a review a week later, and it is also why retention limits matter: the log should outlive the shift, not the year. A case with a timestamped assignment, an arrival and a short written note needs no reconstruction from memory.

03

Patrol

What does patrol check-in add to positioning?

Patrol check-in turns a position stream into a task record. Routes are defined as ordered checkpoints, officers check in at each one by scanning a code, tapping a tag or using a kiosk reader, and the platform records time, sequence and completion. Compared with position alone, check-in is evidence: it proves the officer was at the point, not merely near it.

The two layers work together. Check-in proves presence at a checkpoint. Position fills the gaps between checkpoints and shows whether the officer was where the route said they would be. A missed checkpoint with a position trail through the area usually means a reader problem, and a missed checkpoint with no position trail is a real gap.

Check-in is also the cheapest part of the deployment. It needs tags or printed codes and a handset, not a gateway on every ceiling, so a venue can start with routes and check-ins and add positioning later if a specific question needs the trail between points. The patrol system page covers how completion rate and duration are calculated, and what to do when a route is missed for reasons outside the officer's control.

04

Scoping

How is visibility limited to a shift?

Visibility is tied to the shift, not to the person. When a staff member clocks on, their device becomes visible to their supervisor and to the operations desk. When they clock off, the live view stops. Nothing about the break between shifts is visible unless the venue has explicitly enabled it for a role, and that choice is recorded.

Consent is collected once, in writing, with the purpose stated: dispatch, patrol verification and emergency mustering. It covers issued devices, which is the cleanest arrangement, and it can cover personal handsets only where the staff member agrees and the app makes the tracking state visible on screen at all times.

Retention is the second half of the promise. We recommend a short window for detailed position history, a longer window for patrol and incident records, and deletion of the detailed stream on schedule. A retention policy that is not enforced by the software is a policy that will be quietly broken during the first busy month.

  • Live view enabled only during a clocked-on shift
  • Consent recorded per person, with the stated purpose
  • Tracking state visible on the staff device at all times
  • Separate retention for position history and for case records
  • Automatic deletion on schedule, not by manual clean-up

05

Access

Who can see what?

Access should follow the org chart, not convenience. A shift supervisor sees their own team during their own shift. The operations desk sees all on-duty staff during operating hours. A duty manager sees the same view plus alerts. Human resources should not have a live view at all; if they need data for a specific case, they get a defined extract, not a login.

History playback is the role that needs the most care, because it reconstructs a shift after the fact. It is used for incident review, for verifying a patrol gap and for training. It should be limited to named roles, logged on every use, and capped at a variable playback speed so a long shift can be reviewed quickly without skipping the interesting part.

Every view of a person's trail should be attributable. If the platform cannot say who watched which trail, when, and under which case, it is not ready to be deployed in a workplace with a works council or a union agreement. That single audit requirement is usually enough to settle the argument about whether history belongs in the first phase.

06

Integrations

How does it connect to access control and the fire alarm?

Access control tells you who entered a controlled area and when. Positioning tells you who is nearby and available. Joined together, a door forced event can be dispatched to the nearest officer without a radio round, and a badge read at a restricted door can be checked against the fence rules on that zone.

The fire alarm integration is the one operators value most after a real evacuation. When an alarm zone activates, the platform can list staff devices last seen in that zone, by team, and show mustering progress at each assembly point. That is a list of likely occupants, not a guaranteed occupancy, and it must be presented that way to a warden: a phone left on a desk will appear as a person.

Both integrations need the same discipline. Map the external system's zones and doors to the venue map once, keep the mapping under version control, and retest after any building change. A door that was renumbered during a refit will silently break the dispatch path.

FAQ

Common questions

Can't see your question? Send it with your floor count and we answer within one working day.

Ask a specific question

Can staff be tracked outside their shift?

No, by design. Live visibility starts at clock-on and stops at clock-off unless a venue has explicitly enabled an exception for a role such as on-call engineering, and that exception is recorded and shown to the staff member. Our recommendation is to keep exceptions rare and time limited.

Does it use cameras to identify staff?

No. Position comes from the device the staff member carries, using BLE, UWB or Wi-Fi positioning. There is no facial recognition, no biometric identification and no identity inference from video in this layer. Where a venue runs CCTV, that system is separate and separately governed.

How accurate is staff positioning indoors?

Typical horizontal accuracy is 1 to 3 m with standard BLE, 1 to 2 m with a hybrid BLE, Wi-Fi and map matching approach, and 0.1 to 0.3 m with UWB. Accuracy is site dependent and is confirmed during commissioning, since it changes with ceiling height, wall materials and density of anchors.

Can a union or works council review the design?

They should. The design is easier to defend when it is concrete: which devices, which purpose, which roles can view, what retention applies and what the platform cannot do. We can supply a written description of the data flow for that review, and the retention and role settings are configurable to whatever is agreed. A design that depends on nobody asking a hard question is the one that fails that review.

Is the position history needed at all?

Not for every venue. Live dispatch and check-in work without history. History is used for incident review, patrol verification and training. If a venue does not need it, disabling playback removes the most sensitive feature at no operational cost. Where it is enabled, restrict it to named roles, log every view and keep the retention window short.

What happens during an evacuation?

When an alarm zone activates, the platform can list the staff devices last seen in that zone and show mustering progress at each assembly point. Wardens should treat the list as likely occupants rather than a definitive headcount, because a device left behind still reports a position.

Which devices can staff carry?

Issued badges or tags give the most consistent data and the clearest consent position. A handset running the venue app works for staff who already carry one, provided the tracking state is visible on screen. Fixed terminals are an option for lobbies and plant rooms, but they do not follow a person.
