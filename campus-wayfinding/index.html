Campuses

Campus wayfinding across buildings, wings, floors and a room numbering system nobody loves

We model the space hierarchy once, link it to the student portal, and republish when the timetable changes the map.

01

The address model

Why is campus wayfinding mostly a numbering problem?

Ask a first-year student to find room 2.14 and the answer depends on which building they are in. Some blocks number by floor and door, some continue a corridor sequence across levels, some use a letter for the wing followed by a number with no relation to the floor. Rooms are renamed when a department moves, and the old code survives in timetables, email signatures and door plates for years afterward. The map has to hold the current code and the aliases people still search for.

Buildings compound it. A campus is rarely a set of rectangles: there are linked blocks, a wing reachable only through another building, a lecture theatre entered from the floor below the one it is named after. Any of these breaks a simple address model, and a map built on a simple address model sends people confidently to the right door of the wrong block.

  • Numbering schemes differ between blocks and predate current use
  • Old room codes survive in timetables and email for years
  • Linked blocks and wing entries break simple address models

02

Data model

How do you model buildings, wings, floors and rooms?

We build a space hierarchy rather than a flat list of pins: campus, building, wing or zone, floor, room. Every room inherits its position from the hierarchy, so a corridor is drawn once and serves every room off it, and a single floor redraw touches one level. Each room record carries a display name, a short code, the owning department, the room type and any alias codes that people still use in practice.

Data comes from whatever the estate already holds: CAD floor plans, a space management export, a timetabling room list. Those three rarely agree, so the first week is reconciliation rather than drawing. We return a room list with conflicts flagged, which is usually the first time anyone has compared the timetabling codes against the space register in one table.

03

Change cycle

What changes every semester, and how is it handled?

Semester change is a scheduled data event, not a surprise. Rooms change hands, a lecture moves to a larger theatre, an exam hall becomes a temporary venue for two weeks, a department takes over a floor. Because the map is data, the change is a batch update to the room records plus a publish, not a redraw. A department move that used to wait six weeks for new signs can be live before the first lecture of term.

The student portal is the natural distribution point. A timetable entry carries the room code, so a link from the portal can open the route already aimed at that room, with the building entrance, the correct wing and the floor change resolved. Students arrive at the room instead of at a reception desk asking where the room is, which is the workload that lands on front desks during the first week of every term.

  • Semester change is a batch data update plus a publish
  • Alias codes kept so legacy room numbers still resolve in search
  • Portal link opens the route aimed at the timetabled room
  • Temporary venues added and removed without a redraw
  • Same map inside the campus app or mini program

04

Visitor mode

Does it hold up on open days and campus tours?

Open days invert the normal pattern. Visitors outnumber regular users, they arrive by car or train, and they follow a printed program rather than a timetable. A visitor mode gives them a short destination list, temporary POIs for stands and talks, and a route from the car park or station rather than from a hall they already know. QR posters at the entrance and on the program open the map without an install, which matters for parents using a phone they are not used to.

Tours use the same data differently. A guided route publishes as a sequence, and location sharing by link lets a guide send a live position to a late arrival. Because the temporary layout is a layer above the base campus, it is removed on Monday without touching the permanent rooms underneath.

  • Visitor mode with a short destination list, not the full estate
  • Temporary stand and talk POIs as a dated layer
  • QR on the printed program opens the map without an install
  • Guided tour sequence plus live position sharing by link

05

Arrival

Where do parking and vehicle access fit?

Most campus arrivals are by car, and the last three hundred meters is where the frustration lives. Parking guidance covers space search, guidance to a free bay and reverse car finder by plate number or bay number, with bay reservation tied to plate recognition where the site runs numbered zones. On a campus with permit areas, the useful part is usually the walk from the car park to the building, including the step-free option from the accessible bays.

Bus, bicycle and pedestrian arrivals deserve the same treatment. A map that only knows main entrances and car parks ignores the two or three arrival points that most students actually use, and those are precisely the places where a new student gets lost in week one.

  • Free bay search and guidance, reverse finder by plate or bay
  • Step-free walk from accessible bays to the building door
  • Bus stops, cycle racks and side gates modelled as entries

06

Egress

How do emergency assembly points work on the map?

Assembly points are POIs with a role, not a separate system. Each zone is drawn as an indoor geofence, the assembly point is published as an egress destination, and a fire alarm or emergency integration switches the map into egress mode so routes lead out to the assigned point rather than through the building. Zone entry and exit events can raise configurable alerts for wardens, by message, voice or trigger count.

Staff positioning supports the same event from the other side: live staff position on the 2D or 3D map, dispatch to a zone, and patrol check-in with route and compliance records. It runs on consenting staff devices or badges during shifts and does not identify students or visitors, and there is no camera-based identity tracking in the platform.

  • Assembly points published as egress destinations
  • Fire alarm integration switches the map into egress mode
  • Zone entry and exit alerts for wardens, configurable per zone
  • Staff dispatch on consenting badges during shifts only

07

Schedule

How long does a campus rollout take?

One building is a short project and a whole campus is a program. The reference schedule per building is six to ten weeks: survey three to five days, map production two to three weeks, platform setup and portal integration two to four weeks, calibration and training one week. It is typical rather than fixed, because survey access to teaching space has to fit around the teaching calendar, and a lab block is slower to survey than a lecture hall.

The portal integration is usually the longest single item on a campus, not the mapping. Matching timetable room codes to physical rooms exposes every inconsistency in the estate at once, and fixing those records is often more valuable than the map itself. Budget that reconciliation before you budget the kiosks.

  • 6-10 weeks per building, typical and confirmed after survey
  • Portal code reconciliation is the long pole, not the drawing
  • Survey access follows the teaching calendar, not the project plan

08

Sequence

Phasing a rollout building by building

Start where the complaints are. On most campuses that is the building with the most first-year lectures, or the one where the room numbering is worst, because those generate the front desk workload the project is meant to remove. Prove the update workflow with the team that will maintain it, then extend in groups by walking distance so that cross-building routes work as soon as two adjacent blocks are mapped.

Keep the publishing discipline from day one. One named owner for room data, one weekly publish, one emergency path for room closures. The failure mode on campuses is not bad software, it is a map that was accurate in September and quietly wrong by March because nobody owned the April changes.

  • Begin with the building generating the most wayfinding complaints
  • Extend by walking distance so cross-building routes work early
  • One named room data owner and one weekly publish window

FAQ

Common questions

Can't see your question? Send it with your floor count and we answer within one working day.

Ask a specific question

Can campus wayfinding use our existing room numbers as they are?

Yes. We keep the codes the estate already uses and store them as aliases alongside a display name, so a search for an old code still resolves. Room numbering schemes are not standardised for us, they are recorded as found. Where two systems disagree, we return a conflict list rather than silently choosing one.

How do we keep the map correct when rooms change each semester?

Treat the change as a data update, not a redraw. A named owner edits the room records in the map editor, and a weekly publish pushes the change to kiosks, the portal link and any embedded app. An emergency path covers a room closing mid-term. Because records are edited rather than drawn, a batch of a hundred room changes takes hours rather than weeks.

Can it link from the student portal or the timetable?

Yes. A timetable entry carries a room code, so the portal can pass that code to the map and open a route already aimed at the room, with entrance, wing and floor change resolved. Students do not search, they arrive. The integration is a link and a room code, so it does not require exposing timetable data beyond what the student can already see.

Does it help on open days when visitors arrive by car?

That is often the first use case that proves the map. A visitor mode carries a short destination list, temporary POIs for stands and talks, and a route from the car park or station. QR posters on the printed program and at each entrance open the map in a browser, with no install, which suits parents and school groups using unfamiliar phones.

Can students use it without installing an app?

Yes. Routes open in the phone browser from a QR poster, a link in the portal or a message from student services. A kiosk in the library or student center serves the same map for anyone without a phone to hand. An embedded app or mini program is optional where the institution already runs one.

How does it handle emergency assembly points?

Assembly points are POIs with an egress role. Zones are drawn as indoor geofences, and an alarm integration switches the map into egress mode so routes lead out to the assigned point. Wardens can receive configurable zone alerts. Staff location for dispatch runs only on consenting staff devices during shifts, and no student or visitor is identified.

Do we have to map the whole campus at once?

No, and we usually advise against it. Map the building with the worst wayfinding first, prove the update workflow, then extend in groups of adjacent buildings so cross-building routes work early. A campus mapped in one pass without an owner for room data tends to be accurate in September and wrong by March.
