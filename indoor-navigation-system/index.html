INDOOR NAVIGATION

Indoor navigation system: routes, steps and handover from street to floor

An indoor navigation system routes a visitor from a searched destination to a numbered sequence of steps across floors. It combines a multi-floor map, a walkable route graph and a live position estimate, then guides the visitor through stairs, escalators, elevators or a step-free path, on a phone or on a directory kiosk.

01

Definition

What an indoor navigation system includes

An indoor navigation system turns a multilevel building into a route graph that can be searched, planned and followed step by step. It is not a floor plan with a marker dropped on it. Three things have to stay in step: the map geometry of every floor, the walkable connections between those floors, and a live estimate of where the visitor is standing. When someone searches for a store, a service counter or a ward, the system returns a numbered sequence of steps, each with a distance, a direction and the landmark to look for.

We deliver the full stack rather than one screen. That covers indoor 3D mapping, the routing engine, the phone and web front end, directory kiosks, parking guidance with reverse car finding, and the admin platform that keeps content current. The same platform carries AR wayfinding on the camera view, panorama stills at junctions, people counting and indoor geofencing. Venues usually start with search and routing, then add the modules that match how the building is actually used.

  • Search by tenant name, unit number, room, category or map pin
  • Numbered steps with distance, turn direction and a visible landmark
  • Route shown on a 2D plan, a 3D view or the live camera
  • The same route sent from a kiosk to a phone by QR code
  • Content, floors and languages managed in one admin platform

Routing is one layer; the position estimate underneath it is a separate problem, described under indoor positioning.

02

How does the system plan a route between two points?

Planning starts from a walk graph, not from the drawing. Junctions, doors, lift lobbies, stair landings and destination points become nodes; every corridor, flight, ramp and lift ride becomes an edge. Each edge carries a length, a width, a slope, a floor change and a set of flags such as stairs, staff-only or temporarily closed. A weighted graph search then returns the cheapest path under the weights the venue has chosen.

The weights are where a venue policy lives. Distance is the obvious cost, but a floor change, a heavy fire door, an escalator that runs one way before 10:00 or a corridor closed by a shutter at night all change the answer. We set those costs with your operations team while the map is produced, inside the editor, so the routing behaves like the building rather than like a generic grid.

Travel time is estimated from edge length at a walking speed of about 1.2 m per second, plus an allowance for waiting at a lift or standing on an escalator. The estimate is deliberately conservative, because a route that claims two minutes and takes four reads as broken even when the path itself is correct. Where a venue has measured its own footfall, we replace the default speed with a figure taken from that data, floor by floor.

03

Vertical travel

How does turn-by-turn guidance handle stairs, escalators and elevators?

Vertical transitions are where indoor routes fail in practice, so each element carries its own model. A stair is a flight count with a direction and a landing shape; an escalator has a running direction and operating hours; an elevator has a served floor list, a lobby position and a door side. The step text names the element, the number of flights or the target floor, and the turn to make on arrival.

Guidance does not stop at the lobby. The route continues from the elevator door into the corridor, and the map view switches floor at the moment the step changes. On a phone, the camera overlay picks up the route again on the new floor without a second search. At a junction where two corridors look identical, a panorama still shows the real view and settles the decision faster than another line on a plan. A visitor who takes the wrong turn is not restarted, because the system replans from the estimated position.

Vertical movement modelled per element
ElementHow it is modelledWhat the visitor reads
StairsFlight count, direction and landing shapeTake the stairs on the left up two flights, then turn right
EscalatorRunning direction and hours of operationTake the up escalator beside the pharmacy to level 3
ElevatorServed floors, lobby position and door sideTake elevator B to level 4 and exit to the right
RampGradient and width of each ramp segmentFollow the ramp behind the counter to the lower concourse

If a lift is out of service, the venue suspends it in the editor and every affected route is replanned at the next search.

04

How do routes continue from outdoors to indoors, and across buildings?

Every building we map gets entrance anchors: main door, side doors, service entrances, car park lift lobbies, and the point where a drop-off area meets the pavement. From the street, a route can start on the outdoor site model, follow a path or driveway to the correct door, and hand over to the indoor graph at that anchor with no gap in the step list.

Multi-building sites use the same idea between buildings. A skybridge, a covered walkway, a tunnel or a shared podium becomes an edge that links two buildings on one site, so a clinic appointment in block C is still reachable from block A through a single search. Parking is treated as another level: the route starts at the bay, walks to the lift or the stairs, and continues indoors.

The position source changes as the visitor moves. Outside, the handset location service is usually good enough to place someone on the approach path. Inside, the system switches to the venue position layer, whether that is BLE, UWB, Wi-Fi or a hybrid, and the route keeps the same identifier so nothing restarts. The visitor does not see a switch, but two things change: the accuracy radius around the marker widens or narrows with the source, and the remaining distance recalculates once the indoor estimate settles.

05

Scope note

What is the difference between navigation and positioning?

These two words are used interchangeably in briefs and they should not be. Positioning answers a measurement question: where is this device, on which floor, with what confidence. Navigation answers a planning question: given where the visitor is and where they want to go, what is the ordered set of moves that gets them there. Either one can be bought alone, and a mis-scoped project usually buys one while expecting the other.

The practical consequence is where the survey budget goes. If the walk graph is thin, no amount of radio accuracy produces a sensible step list, because the path through the building is simply not modelled. If the position layer is weak, the step list reads correctly while the marker drifts, which visitors experience as a broken system. We size both from the same site survey.

Navigation and positioning answer different questions
QuestionPositioningNavigation
What it answersWhere is this device right nowHow do I get from here to the destination
What it returnsA coordinate, a floor and a confidence valueOrdered steps with distances, turns and landmarks
How it failsRadio coverage gaps or calibration driftA missing door or corridor link in the walk graph

06

How do you keep routes usable for step-free access?

Step-free routing is a property of the graph, not a switch flipped at the end. Corridors, doors, thresholds and ramps are flagged as accessible or not while the map is produced, using the widths and slopes measured on site. When a visitor asks for a step-free route, the search is restricted to that subset, and every lift becomes a mandatory node rather than an optional shortcut.

The honest trade-off is distance. A step-free route in most buildings is longer than the direct one, sometimes considerably, so the step list states the extra distance and the extra time up front rather than letting the visitor discover it halfway. Accessibility also covers the destination end: a high service counter, a reception with a single step at the entrance, or a washroom door that opens inward should be flagged in the POI data, not left for the visitor to find.

  • Corridor width, door type and slope recorded per edge
  • Ramps and lifts mandatory on every step-free search
  • Extra distance and time shown before the route starts
  • Accessible entrances and counters flagged on the POI

07

What does deployment and calibration take?

A reference venue of 50,000 to 100,000 m2 starts with a survey of 3 to 5 days. Drawing production, POI capture and the routing graph take 2 to 3 weeks. Platform setup and integration with the systems you already run take 2 to 4 weeks, overlapping with production where the content is ready. On-site calibration, walk testing and staff training take about 1 week at the end.

In total that is 6 to 10 weeks phased, and the phase order matters more than the total. Content and the walk graph have to be frozen before calibration, otherwise the walk test measures a map that is still moving. We send the route list for acceptance testing at least a week ahead, so your team picks the destinations that matter rather than the ones that are easiest to test.

Calibration itself has two passes. The first aligns the position layer with the surveyed coordinates on each floor. The second walks the acceptance routes with a real handset and records where the marker leaves the corridor, where the floor changes too early or too late, and where a step instruction is technically correct but reads badly on the ground. Both passes are logged against the same route list, so a route that failed the first pass is retested in the second rather than assumed fixed.

The schedule is typical and is confirmed after the site survey.

08

How do you check that the routes work after go-live?

Acceptance is a walk test against a written list. For each destination, a tester follows the steps from three starting points, records whether the position marker stays on the route, whether the floor switches at the right landing, and whether the arrival point is within a few meters of the door. Failures are logged by step number, which makes the fix a data correction rather than a discussion.

After go-live the platform reports what the routing is actually doing: searches that returned nothing, routes that were started but never finished, destinations that are searched often but rarely reached, and the floors where sessions end early. On a kiosk network the same numbers are available per terminal. That report is usually the first hard evidence that a tenant has moved, a corridor has been closed, or a unit number in the directory no longer matches the signage.

09

Who keeps the map current when tenants change?

Day to day changes belong to the venue. The map editor runs in a browser, and venue staff can add or retire a POI, rename a unit, change opening hours, adjust a search keyword or hide a closed unit without touching code. Changes are drafted, previewed and then published, so a mistake stays in draft until someone with the right role approves it.

Structural changes are different. A new corridor, a demolished wall, a relocated lift or a new building entrance changes the walk graph and needs a short remap, because a flag on a drawing is not the same as a measured connection. We scope remaps by floor or by zone, so the cost stays proportional to what actually changed rather than to the size of the building.

FAQ

Common questions

Ask a specific question

Do visitors need to install an app to use indoor navigation?

No. A route can be opened from a QR code on signage or at a directory kiosk and run in the phone browser, with AR guidance where the handset supports it. The SDK is there for venues that want navigation inside their own app or mini program, and for those venues the experience is identical in content, only the container changes.

How accurate does the position need to be for navigation to feel correct?

A stable floor and a horizontal error inside about 2 m at the seventieth percentile is enough for corridor guidance in a mall. Below that, the marker visibly drifts between parallel corridors and the step text contradicts the map. Precision beyond 1 m is worth paying for when the destination is a single bay or a specific counter, not for general wayfinding.

Can the system route a wheelchair user around every staircase?

Yes, provided the data exists. Step-free routing runs on a restricted set of edges flagged during mapping from measured widths, door types and slopes. Lifts become mandatory nodes on those routes. The step-free path is often longer than the direct one, and the system states the extra distance and time before the visitor starts.

What happens to navigation during a fire alarm?

An alarm interface can switch the kiosks and the venue app to a predefined evacuation view, with exit routes towards the nearest safe exit rather than a selected destination. We plan that behavior with your fire safety officer, because evacuation routing is a life safety function and its logic is agreed with the responsible person, not set by default.

Can a route start from a directory kiosk instead of a phone?

Yes. The visitor searches on the touch screen, sees the route on the large display with the floor directory, and sends it to a phone by scanning a QR code. That handoff is the usual pattern in malls, where most visitors begin at an entrance kiosk rather than opening a site at home.

How many floors and buildings can one system cover?

Up to 20 levels per building, and several buildings on one site linked by bridges, walkways or a shared podium. Above that scale the limit is content management rather than routing: the platform supports multi-venue administration, and we would plan the instance structure with your operations team during survey.

Does navigation work where there is no mobile signal?

The routing engine and the map are served by the platform, so a cached session can continue in a basement or a shielded corridor. Position updates resume when connectivity returns. Kiosks on a wired network are unaffected. We test the dead zones named in the survey during acceptance, since basements are usually where visitors are most lost.

How is an indoor navigation system priced?

Cost follows four drivers: mapped area and floor count, positioning coverage and device count, kiosk or terminal quantity, and integration scope. A single-building venue with phone-only guidance sits at the low end. We return a scope table with quantities and a budgetary proposal within 24 hours of receiving floor plans, with no currency figure committed before the survey.
