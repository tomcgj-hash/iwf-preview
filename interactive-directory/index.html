01

Definition

What is an interactive directory?

An interactive directory is a fixed touch screen that replaces the printed board at an entrance. It shows the venue floor plan in 2D or 3D, a searchable tenant or department list, a floor selector, and a route line that leads from the kiosk to the destination door. A visitor types a name or taps a category, sees where the destination sits on the plan, and either follows the route on the panel or sends it to a phone. Units sit where people stop to orient: main entrance, lift lobby, food court junction, parking lift core. The directory is the fixed point where indoor navigation begins.

The assembly is a commercial touch panel in a floor-standing, wall-mounted or totem enclosure, with an industrial PC or Android board inside and a network link to a server. The server holds the map, the tenant database and the content schedule. When a tenant changes floor or a new brand opens, an editor updates the map and each kiosk pulls the change at the next sync, with no site visit and no software rebuild. That split between panel and content is what makes a directory something a facilities team can operate for years instead of an installation that freezes on the day it is commissioned.

02

Hardware

Which kiosk form factor fits your entrance?

Form factor follows the queue, not the design mood. A portrait floor-standing unit at the main entrance answers the first orientation question in a few seconds and survives a busy Saturday. A landscape unit near an escalator landing gives more tenant columns per screen and suits a visitor who already knows roughly where to go. Wall-mounted units keep corridors clear where the landlord will not allow a footprint on the floor. Totems with a second screen suit open concourses where people approach from two directions.

Every enclosure we specify uses the same software stack, so a venue can mix forms without splitting the content into separate systems. Screen size sets what fits on one screen, not what the platform can do. A 43 inch panel holds a map view and one search result list comfortably. A 55 inch unit shows the map, a longer tenant list and a promotion strip at the same time without asking the visitor to scroll.

03

Content

What is on the screen?

The default layout puts the map in the center, a search field at the top and a category strip along one edge. The visitor can search by name, browse by category, or tap a floor. Selecting a destination draws the route from the kiosk position to the destination door, with the estimated walking time and the accessible alternative if stairs are on the default path. The panel then offers a QR code so the visitor can carry the route on a phone.

Between visits the screen runs an attract loop: a brand film, an event board, a promotion strip or the venue map at a slow pan. When a hand comes near the panel the loop yields to the directory in under a second, and returns after a set idle interval. That idle time is what turns a cost center into an advertising surface, and the same scheduling engine that drives the loop also drives event takeovers during a campaign week.

  • Venue map with floors, categories, unit outlines and landmarks
  • Tenant or department search by name, category or keyword
  • Floor selector with a direct jump to any level
  • Route with walking time, and a step-free alternative
  • Event board and promotion strip during idle time
  • Accessibility mode with larger type and higher contrast

04

Handoff

How does the kiosk hand a route to the visitor's phone?

When the visitor taps the handoff control, the panel shows a QR code tied to that destination and that kiosk. The scan opens the venue app if it is installed, or a web link in the phone browser if it is not, so a visitor never has to install anything to finish the walk. The phone then takes over with turn-by-turn guidance, the AR camera view where the venue provides it, and off-route correction if the visitor drifts into the wrong corridor.

The handoff is a session, not a printout. The kiosk writes the destination and the start point into the platform, the phone reads them back, and the two stay consistent if the tenant has moved floors since the map was last cached. If the venue runs no app and no mini program, the same route can be sent as a shareable link, and the visitor can also share live position with a companion for the length of the walk.

05

Site planning

How do you place, power and network a directory?

Placement follows footfall and hesitation. The strongest positions are the point where a visitor first stops and looks around: just inside the main entrance, at the lift lobby of the busiest tower, at the junction where a concourse splits, and at the lift core that serves the car park. A unit two meters behind a queue forms a bottleneck; a unit set back from the traffic line with a meter of clear space in front gets used. We survey sight lines from the approach direction before fixing a position.

Most units run on a standard 100 to 240 V supply with a dedicated circuit per kiosk, drawn at roughly 100 to 200 W depending on screen size and brightness, plus a small allowance for a heater in an outdoor enclosure. Wired Ethernet is the default because a directory shows video and pulls map tiles, and a cable is one less thing to debug in year three. Where a cable cannot be run, a Wi-Fi link or a cellular router works, provided the unit can reach the server over outbound HTTPS.

  • Entrance, lift lobby and concourse junction before decorative spots
  • One meter of clear standing space in front of the panel
  • Screen center at eye height for the dominant visitor posture
  • Wired Ethernet preferred, Wi-Fi or cellular as a fallback
  • Dedicated circuit per unit, protected from cleaning water
  • Outbound HTTPS to the terminal server, no inbound port on site

Power, data and floor fixing are landlord works in most leases, so the site survey output names the positions, the circuits and the cable routes before procurement starts.

06

Operations

How does one server manage a whole estate?

The terminal server is the control point. One server can manage up to 100 directory terminals, across one venue or a portfolio, and each terminal appears in a list with its status, its last sync time and its screen version. Content publishes from the server: a tenant change, a floor rename, a campaign takeover or an emergency message is pushed to the selected terminals at the next poll, and a single unit can be excluded when it is being serviced.

Remote management covers the failures that actually happen. A panel that has lost its network, a player that has stopped rendering, a storage partition that is filling with logs: each raises a state on the dashboard rather than waiting for a visitor to complain. A remote reboot clears most stalls without a site visit. Software updates ship as a signed package, and a venue can hold a group of terminals on the previous version while the rest of the estate moves forward.

  • Up to 100 terminals per server, grouped by venue, floor or zone
  • Content scheduling by date, time and terminal group
  • Health states for network loss, player stall and storage
  • Remote reboot and remote screenshot for support
  • Staged software updates with a rollback path
  • Audit trail of who published which content and when

07

Options

Do you need an RFID, NFC or barcode reader?

A reader turns the kiosk into an identity touchpoint. With an RFID or NFC reader fitted behind the panel, a staff badge or a visitor pass taps the screen and the unit checks the holder against a list, marks attendance, or prints a route to a booked room. A barcode or QR reader at the front does the same job for a printed appointment slip, which is common in outpatient halls where a patient arrives with a paper referral.

The reader is a hardware option, not a tracking feature. We fit it because venues already issue badges and passes and want the directory to confirm a check-in, not because the kiosk needs identity to guide a visitor. If a venue does not issue credentials, a plain touch unit is the right specification, and adding a reader only adds a support surface nobody uses.

  • RFID or NFC badge tap for staff check-in and visitor passes
  • Barcode and QR scanning for printed appointment slips
  • Card list managed on the server, no local card file
  • Optional printer for a paper route slip
  • No biometric capture on any configuration

08

Budget

What drives cost and how long does delivery take?

Cost is set by four things: how many terminals, which enclosure and panel specification, how much of the map and tenant data we build, and how many integrations the venue wants. A single-floor clinic with six departments and one kiosk is a small job. A six-floor mall with two parking levels, 220 tenant points of interest and a link to the parking system is a different scope, and the difference sits in map production and integration hours more than in the panel.

Our reference schedule is typical and confirmed after survey. Survey runs 3 to 5 days for a 50,000 to 100,000 m2 site. Map production takes 2 to 3 weeks. Platform setup and integration take 2 to 4 weeks. On-site calibration and training take 1 week. Phased delivery lands at 6 to 10 weeks, with the first terminals live before the rest of the estate is finished so the venue can test with real visitors.

09

Trade-offs

When is a touch screen the wrong choice?

A touch screen is the wrong buy in a building with one floor, fewer than twenty tenants and no plan to change them. A printed board at the door costs a fraction of the unit, needs no power, no network and no software license, and answers the same question. We say so when the survey points that way, because a venue that over-buys on hardware tends to under-maintain the content, and an out-of-date directory is worse than a printed board.

The second case is low footfall combined with high cleaning load. A panel that is wiped twenty times a day with a wet cloth in a food hall needs a sealed front and a cleaning routine agreed with the operator; where that cannot be guaranteed, a non-touch digital directory board in the same enclosure gives the map and the tenant list with no glass to touch. Touch also loses to print on temporary events: a poster for a two week market is cheaper than a campaign build.

FAQ

Common questions

Can't see your question? Send it with your floor count and we answer within one working day.

Ask a specific question

How many kiosks does a mall actually need?

For a 100,000 m2 six-floor mall, a planning reference is 10 to 16 units: one or two at the main entrance, one per lift lobby, one at each concourse junction and one at the parking lift core. Fewer units push visitors to the entrance to ask again. More units add cleaning and content work without adding reach. We size the count from footfall lines in the survey.

Can an existing printed directory be converted?

Yes, and it is often the cheapest start. We reuse the existing floor plans and tenant list if the landlord holds them as drawings or as a tenant register, redraw them into the map editor, and keep the same position in the lobby. If the panel position already has power and a data point, the hardware swap is a morning of work per unit.

Does the kiosk work without internet access?

It runs a cached map and tenant list from local storage, so search and on-screen routing keep working during a network outage. The QR handoff needs the phone to reach the platform, so it pauses while the link is down and resumes at the next sync. Venues with strict network policy often run the terminal server on site to keep the kiosk independent of the wide area link.

How current can the tenant list be?

A tenant change goes into the map editor on the server and reaches the terminals at the next poll. In practice a venue can publish a new brand, a floor move or a closed unit the same day, and a seasonal campaign can be scheduled weeks ahead with a start and end time. No site visit and no software rebuild is involved for a content change.

Is the touch screen readable in a bright glazed lobby?

With the right panel it is. A 400 nit panel is fine in a shaded interior, while a lobby with a glass wall facing the sun needs 700 nits and an anti-glare coating, and sometimes a baffle or a change of position. We measure the light level at the intended spot during the survey rather than taking the brightest panel as a default.

What happens if a terminal fails during opening hours?

The other terminals keep serving, and the failed unit shows on the server dashboard with its last sync time and network state. Most stalls clear with a remote reboot. Where the fault is the panel or the touch layer, the enclosure opens from the front with a key, and the player module can be swapped without removing the housing from the wall.

Can the kiosk show advertising as well as wayfinding?

Yes. The idle loop carries brand promotion, event boards and venue messages, and the schedule can reserve a share of the loop for paying advertisers while the visitor is not touching the screen. The directory takes priority the moment a hand approaches, so the promotion never blocks the primary job of finding a shop or a clinic.

Do we have to buy the kiosks from you?

No. Venues with a preferred hardware supplier can run our software on their own panel, provided the enclosure meets the touch, sealing and ventilation requirements in the specification. We also supply the units where a single warranty and a single delivery date matter more than a hardware brand already on the approved list.
