Airports

Airport wayfinding from curb to gate, and everything that changes in between

An airport route is judged in minutes, not turns. We map landside, security and airside as one network so a passenger can go from curb to check-in to gate, or from an inbound gate to a tight connection, with walking time shown before they commit. Gate changes republish the same day.

01

The boundary problem

Why is airport wayfinding different from a building directory?

In a mall a wrong turn costs two minutes. In an airport it can cost a connection, a rebooked flight and a night in an airport hotel. The building is also split by a security boundary that most visitors cross once and never learn: landside retail, the screening line, then the sterile airside zone where the gate logic lives. A directory that treats the terminal as one continuous space will route people through walls the building actually enforces, and the passenger discovers the error at the screening queue.

The second difference is that the airport's interior geography changes faster than its walls. Gates are reassigned, an airline swaps terminals, a pier closes for works, a retail unit opens in what used to be a corridor. Signage on the pier falls out of date within days of a gate change, and the passenger reading the old board walks toward a gate that now belongs to another flight. Wayfinding in an airport is a publishing problem as much as a mapping one.

  • Landside, screening and airside are separate zones with real walls
  • Gate reassignment is a daily event, terminal moves are seasonal
  • Passengers cross the security boundary once and never learn it

02

Journey model

How does a passenger get from the curb to the gate?

The journey is a chain of stops, and each stop answers a different question. At the curb the question is which entrance and which check-in row. At the screening line it is which lane and how long. Airside it is the gate, the pier and the level. Arrivals is the opposite chain: gate, immigration, carousel, ground transport. We model the stops rather than the building sections, because a passenger experiences the terminal as a sequence of decisions.

Each stop is a POI with a role, and the route between stops uses the same network as the rest of the map. That single model is why an airport can show a route to a gate, a route to a carousel and a transfer route from the same dataset, and why a gate change updates every channel at once instead of requiring a new printed plan.

03

Transit routing

How do you route a passenger on a tight connection?

A transfer passenger is not an arriving passenger. They are already inside the sterile zone, they hold a fixed onward slot, and their only real question is whether they can walk the distance in the time available. The route starts at the inbound gate rather than at a terminal entrance, follows the sterile path so nobody is sent back through immigration where the airport allows airside transfer, and shows walking time at the start rather than after the first turn.

The comparison the passenger makes is walking time against time remaining. We show distance, floor changes and an honest estimate that assumes normal walking speed with no stops. We do not promise a connection, because the minimum connect time is the operator's rule and not ours. Where a route includes an inter-pier train or a people mover, the wait is a variable, and the map says so instead of pretending the walk is short.

  • Route starts at the inbound gate, not at a terminal entrance
  • Sterile path used where airside transfer is permitted
  • Walking time and floor changes shown before the first turn
  • No connection guarantee, minimum connect time stays with the operator
  • Late passengers get the shortest verified route and nearest boarding point

04

Kiosk placement

Where should kiosks sit in a high-footfall zone?

High-footfall zones are hostile to screens. Concourse kiosks sit in the path of trolleys, get leaned on, and are asked to stay legible under a skylight at midday and again at night. The practical specification is a floor-standing or wall-mounted touch unit with a bright anti-glare panel, hardened front glass, and a mounting that keeps the screen out of the main flow line while staying visible from it. Placement beats count: one kiosk at the junction where the gate concourse splits does more than three in a row at the entrance.

Uptime is the other constraint. One terminal server can manage up to 100 directory terminals, so a large estate becomes a single management surface with remote content pushes, scheduled reboots and status monitoring. We size the count from peak footfall and then confirm it after a pilot week, because the busiest position is rarely the one the plan predicted.

  • Anti-glare panel and hardened glass for skylit concourses
  • Mount out of the flow line but visible from it
  • One server manages up to 100 directory terminals
  • Remote content push, scheduled reboot and status monitoring

05

Multi-building

How does routing work across terminals and levels?

Airports are the venue type where cross-building routing earns its keep. A terminal pair joined by a train, a satellite pier reached by an underground walkway, a landside station connected to a check-in hall by an escalator stack: each is a separate building in map terms and a single journey in passenger terms. The map models the connection as an edge with a walk time, so the route continues across the boundary instead of stopping at a door and asking the passenger to start again.

Vertical routing is equally unforgiving. Departure and arrival flows often occupy the same footprint on different levels, so a passenger who takes the wrong lift ends up on the wrong side of a wall with no way back except the long way. We map level changes explicitly, record which lifts serve which zone, and mark the floor change on the route rather than assuming the reader understands the numbering.

  • Inter-terminal trains and walkways modelled as timed edges
  • Outdoor-to-indoor handover at stations and car parks
  • Lifts mapped per zone, so a route never crosses a sealed wall
  • Level changes called out on the route, not left to inference

06

Publishing

What happens when a gate or an airline moves?

Gate reassignment is a daily event and a terminal move is a seasonal one. Stationary boards handle neither well: the airport either reprints or accepts a period of wrong information. A digital layer changes the gate POI and republishes. Kiosks, the mobile route and any embedded terminal app read the same map, so a passenger who searches a gate or a flight number gets the current location after the publish rather than after the next print run.

The realistic expectation should be stated at the start. Same-day publishing is achievable for a gate change, and two to five working days for a structural change such as a closed pier or a new retail unit, because those need survey and remapping rather than a POI edit. Saying that plainly prevents the common disappointment where an airport assumes a floor plan change is a five-minute job.

  • Gate change is a POI edit and a publish, same day
  • Structural change needs survey, two to five working days
  • One map feeds kiosks, web route and embedded app

07

Language coverage

How many languages does an international airport need?

An international airport usually needs more languages than its signage budget allows, and the gap shows at the kiosk first. The interface and content support any Latin-script language plus Arabic with right-to-left layout, Chinese, Japanese and Korean, with each venue's content managed separately, so a second terminal can carry a different set without forking the product.

The practical discipline is to pick languages from the passenger mix rather than from aspiration, and to keep translated place names in the directory rather than baked into a screenshot. A kiosk with a translated interface and untranslated gate names is worse than a kiosk in a single language, because it signals support it does not have.

  • Per-venue language sets, so terminals can differ
  • Right-to-left Arabic layout supported natively
  • Place names translated in the directory, not in images

08

Schedule

How do you plan and phase an airport deployment?

Airport projects run longer than the map work implies, because survey teams need airside access, escorting and out-of-hours windows, and because any change touching passenger flow goes through an operational review. The reference schedule for one terminal stays at six to ten weeks: survey three to five days, map production two to three weeks, platform setup and integration two to four weeks, calibration and training one week. It is typical, and confirmed after survey.

Phase by passenger journey rather than by building where possible. Landside check-in and the gate concourse first, transfer routing second, arrivals and ground transport last. Each phase is useful on its own, and the first gives the operations team a live dataset to argue about before the harder airside work begins.

  • Airside access drives the schedule more than floor area
  • Journey-based phasing beats building-based phasing
  • Each phase stands alone and is usable on day one

FAQ

Common questions

Can't see your question? Send it with your floor count and we answer within one working day.

Ask a specific question

Can airport wayfinding route a transfer passenger without leaving airside?

Yes, where the airport allows airside transfer. The route starts at the inbound gate and follows the sterile path to the onward departure zone, so the passenger is not sent back through immigration or arrivals. Where a sterile path does not exist, the route follows the official transit flow and flags the border step as part of the journey.

How quickly can a gate change appear on the kiosks?

A gate reassignment is a POI edit, so it can publish the same day once the operations team confirms the change. Structural changes such as a closed pier, a new retail unit or a remapped concourse need a survey and two to five working days. Both figures depend on a published map being maintained by the airport rather than by us.

Does the system need a live flight data feed?

It works without one. Gates, check-in rows and carousels are POIs that can be edited manually and published. A flight feed makes the update faster for high-change airports, but it is optional, and the map stays correct as long as whoever owns gate allocation edits the POI when it moves. No passenger data is read either way.

Will indoor positioning work in a steel and glass terminal?

It works, with caveats. Steel and glass reflect signals, so accuracy depends on beacon density, map matching and calibration rather than on the on-site floor plan alone. Typical results are 1 to 3 meters with BLE and 1 to 2 meters with a hybrid BLE, Wi-Fi and map matching setup. We survey and report measured accuracy in the actual concourse before committing to a design.

How many kiosks does a terminal concourse need?

Fewer than most plans assume, placed better. One kiosk at each junction where a gate concourse splits serves more passengers than a row at the entrance. We size from peak footfall, then confirm from the first pilot week of use, since the busiest position is usually not where the drawing predicted it. One server manages up to 100 terminals.

Can it route passengers who need step-free access?

Yes. Step-free is a route profile on the same map, using lift, ramp and door-width attributes instead of stairs and escalators. Assistance desks and accessible security lanes are POIs on the route, and a lift taken out of service can be flagged so the route recalculates around it rather than sending the passenger to a dead end.

Do passengers need to install an app at the airport?

No. The route opens from a QR code, a link on the boarding pass or a kiosk in the browser, which matters for international visitors who will not install an app for a two-hour transit. An airline or airport app can embed the same map through the SDK where one already exists and is already installed.
