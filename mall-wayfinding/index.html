MALL WAYFINDING

Mall wayfinding: what a deployment actually covers

A mall wayfinding deployment is four layers on one map: a 3D multi-floor map, directory kiosks with tenant search, turn-by-turn navigation that hands the route to the shopper's phone, and parking guidance with a reverse car finder. Each layer has its own hardware, content owner and budget line, which is why most malls phase them.

01

Scope

What does a mall wayfinding deployment actually cover?

A mall wayfinding project is not one product. It is a set of layers that all read the same 3D multi-floor map: the directory kiosks in the concourse, the routing engine behind the QR code, the AR view on a phone camera, the parking guidance above the bays and the reverse car finder at the lift lobby. The layers share a tenant list, a floor model and a naming convention. When those three are inconsistent, every layer above them fails in a visible way, usually in front of shoppers.

The practical consequence is sequencing. Most operators start with the map and the directory, because those two deliver value on day one and force the tenant list to be cleaned. Navigation is added once the map is signed off by leasing. Parking guidance is often a separate contract with the parking contractor even when it appears on the same kiosk screen. Promotion and event content comes last, because it needs an owner and a publishing routine more than it needs technology.

02

Directory layer

How does the directory and kiosk layer work?

The directory kiosk is the part shoppers touch, so it carries most of the perceived quality. Our kiosks run a floor browser, a tenant search with category matching and tolerance for spelling variants, an amenity index for toilets, lifts, ATMs and service desks, and a map that zooms from the whole center down to a single unit. Search dominates real usage, so the index matters more than the graphics: every tenant needs its local names, common misspellings and category synonyms loaded once and maintained in the map editor without a code release.

Placement decides whether the kiosk is used at all. Two or three units at each main entrance catch visitors in the first minute, while they are still disoriented. A second set at anchor junctions and at the top of each escalator catches the mid-trip query. A kiosk beside a plant or behind a queue is decoration. Plan power, data and mounting before ordering hardware, because reopening a finished concourse floor tile is the expensive part of a late decision.

  • Floor browser with unit numbers and a category index
  • Tenant search tolerant of spelling variants and local names
  • Event poster slot plus a large screen loop for quiet hours
  • Optional RFID, NFC or barcode reader for staff check-in
  • One terminal server manages up to 100 directory terminals

A 100,000 m2, six-floor reference configuration uses 10 to 16 kiosks and 220 tenant points of interest, planned as a budgeting example rather than a rule.

03

Promotion layer

How do tenant and event promotions work inside the directory?

Promotion is where the directory stops being a map and starts being a media surface. A category or business district recommendation moves a shopper from a search result for footwear to the three units that stock it on the same level, which shortens the walk and raises the chance of a second stop. Store detail pages carry opening hours, a contact number, a promotion banner and, where the tenant supplies them, a VR panorama or a 720 degree still of the shopfront so the visitor recognises the door before arriving.

Event posters and the large screen loop are usually run by marketing, not by facilities. That split matters at handover: the wayfinding contract should define the template, the image sizes and the publishing route, then hand the routine to the marketing team. If marketing cannot publish without calling an engineer, the screens will still be showing last season's campaign within a quarter, and the kiosk will be read as stale signage rather than as a service.

04

Navigation layer

How does navigation routing work from the kiosk to the shopper's phone?

The handover from kiosk to phone is the detail that makes indoor navigation usable. The kiosk shows the route to the unit, and a QR code carries that same route to the shopper's phone, so nobody is memorising four turns while walking. On the phone the route is turn by turn, with stairs, escalators, lifts and step-free alternatives described separately, because a parent with a stroller and a visitor with a suitcase need different answers for the same two floors.

Cross-building and outdoor-to-indoor handover matter in formats with separated blocks, a podium under towers, or an open-air retail street. The route should continue across the plaza rather than stopping at a door. At junctions where geometry is genuinely ambiguous, a VR panorama of the actual corner removes guesswork that a top-down arrow cannot resolve. Off-route correction brings the walker back onto the path without restarting from the entrance, which is the difference between a usable system and a demo.

  • Route continues across the plaza and between separate blocks
  • Step-free routing as a first-class option, not an afterthought
  • Off-route correction returns the walker to the nearest junction
  • Route can be sent to a second phone for a group meeting point

05

Parking layer

How do parking guidance and the reverse car finder connect to the mall map?

Parking is the layer most often bought separately, and most often regretted as a separate purchase. Entrance guidance with bay counters by level answers the first question a driver has. Guidance to a free bay answers the second. The step that joins parking to the rest of the venue is the pedestrian route from the bay to the lift lobby and from the lobby to the destination floor, drawn on the same map the shopper already met at the entrance kiosk.

The reverse car finder closes the loop. A visitor who cannot recall the level, the zone or the stall can search by plate with fuzzy matching, or by bay number from the ticket, and receive a pedestrian route back to the car. Bay reservation tied to the parking system's plate recognition lets a booked bay open for the booked plate at the barrier, which removes the manual check at the entrance lane and the argument at the exit lane.

Parking guidance, reservation and the reverse car finder are described in detail on the parking guidance system page.

06

Measurement

How do you measure whether mall wayfinding is working?

Measure the layers separately, because a single number hides which layer failed. Search volume tells you whether the kiosks are found and trusted. The share of searches that end in a route request tells you whether the indexing is good enough to answer the question. Routes completed against routes started tells you whether the routing survives contact with the building. Top destinations tell leasing which units the map is actually feeding.

Dwell proxies need a caveat. A geofence entry and exit shows that a device stayed inside a zone for some seconds; it does not show that anyone bought anything. Treat it as a signal about the route ending in a visit rather than a pass, then compare it with the tenant's own figures before drawing a conclusion. Combined with counter data, the picture is useful. On its own, a dwell number invites over-reading.

07

Return model

How should you build a return model before you spend?

Return on a wayfinding deployment is a model, not a number anyone can hand you. Four inputs drive the service half: visitor sessions per day, the share of visitors who ask a person for directions, the minutes each query costs a staff member, and the share of those queries that self-service replaces. Multiply the first three, apply the fourth, and you have saved staff minutes per day. Convert them with your own loaded hourly labor figure.

The commercial half is separate. If a business district recommendation moves a share of searches to a second storey, the value is incremental tenant visits multiplied by your average basket and your own conversion assumption. Both halves run on your figures rather than ours. We will build the worksheet with you and label every assumption, including the ones that turn out wrong, so the decision is about the assumptions rather than about a promised percentage.

  • Assumption 1: visitor sessions per day, from your counters
  • Assumption 2: share of visitors who ask staff for directions
  • Assumption 3: staff minutes lost per direction query
  • Assumption 4: share of queries replaced by self-service
  • Assumption 5: incremental tenant visits per recommendation served

If two of the five assumptions are uncertain, the model still works. It shows which assumption to test first with a one-month pilot.

08

Schedule

What does a mall deployment schedule look like?

The reference schedule is phased and it is planning guidance, not a commitment. A survey takes 3 to 5 days for a venue of 50,000 to 100,000 m2. Map production takes 2 to 3 weeks, and it is the stage that waits on you, because it needs signed floor drawings and a frozen tenant list. Platform setup and integration take 2 to 4 weeks. On-site calibration and training take about one week. The total is 6 to 10 weeks when the phases overlap cleanly.

Two things stretch that window in practice: floor drawings that disagree with the building, and a tenant list that changes during map production. Both are cheap to fix before week two and expensive afterwards, because a map change after calibration means a return visit. Ask leasing to freeze the list for the production window, and accept that minor changes can be made later in the map editor.

The schedule is typical and is confirmed after the survey, once the drawing set and floor count are known.

09

Pitfalls

What usually goes wrong on a mall wayfinding project?

The failures are rarely in the routing engine. They are in data, placement and ownership. A tenant list maintained by three departments produces duplicate units and dead entries within months. Floor naming that differs between the drawing set, the lifts and the directory confuses visitors who read all three. Kiosks placed by symmetry rather than by traffic pattern sit unused, and the project is then judged on the least useful terminal in the building.

Ownership is the quiet one. After handover, someone has to own the tenant data, someone has to own the promotion content, and someone has to own the hardware. If that lands on a facilities team with no content routine, the system degrades to a static map within one or two lease cycles. Agree the three owners before the contract is signed, and write the update routine into the handover.

  • Freeze the tenant list during map production, not after
  • Use one floor naming convention across drawings, lifts and screens
  • Place kiosks at entrances and anchor junctions, not by symmetry
  • Name an owner for tenant data, promotion content and hardware
  • Keep a change log so map edits can be audited later

FAQ

Common questions

Can't see your question? Send it with your floor count and we answer within one working day.

Ask a specific question

How long does a mall wayfinding project take?

The reference schedule runs 6 to 10 weeks: 3 to 5 days of survey for a venue between 50,000 and 100,000 m2, 2 to 3 weeks of map production, 2 to 4 weeks of platform setup and integration, and about a week of calibration and training. The window is typical and is confirmed after the survey, once the floor count and drawing set are known.

Do we need positioning hardware in every corridor?

No. Beacon density follows corridor width, ceiling height and the accuracy you actually need. Kiosk routing works without any positioning hardware, because the kiosk knows where it stands. Phone navigation needs gateways along the walking paths and around junctions, not in store rooms, back-of-house or areas visitors never enter.

Can the system use the tenant list we already maintain?

Yes, and it usually should. We import your list into the map editor and keep your identifiers, then add the fields the directory needs: category, local names, common misspellings and amenity tags. Duplicate or retired units are flagged during import so leasing can close them before the map goes live rather than after.

How many kiosks does a mall actually need?

Two or three per main entrance plus a set at anchor junctions and the top of each escalator. The 100,000 m2 six-floor reference configuration uses 10 to 16 terminals, planned as a budgeting example. The count follows the number of decision points a visitor crosses, not the floor area alone.

Does it work in an open-air center or one with separate blocks?

Yes. Routes continue across plazas and between separate blocks, and the map includes outdoor site modeling so the handover from street to concourse does not break. In those layouts the outdoor stretch usually needs its own signage, and the QR poster at the block entrance is what carries the route across.

Can we start with the map and add navigation later?

That is the common sequence, and it is a reasonable one. The map and directory can go live without positioning hardware, and the same map then supports navigation, AR and parking layers when they are funded. Nothing is rebuilt: the tenant data and floor model carry forward, which is why map quality is worth insisting on early.

Who keeps the tenant data current after handover?

Whoever you assign. The map editor allows tenant and POI changes without a code release, so a leasing coordinator can add or retire a unit the same day. The failure mode is assigning that work to a team with no routine. Name the owner and the review cadence in the handover document.

Does the system track shoppers individually?

No. Measurement uses aggregated search, route and zone events, plus sensor counters for people counting. There is no camera-based identity tracking and no biometric identification. Staff positioning covers consenting staff devices or badges during shifts, and is used for dispatch, patrol and emergency response.

How do step-free and accessibility routes work?

Step-free routing is a routing mode, not a separate map. The walker selects it and the engine avoids stairs, preferring lifts and ramps, then describes the route in the same turn-by-turn form. Lifts are treated as floors with waiting points, so the instruction reads as take the lift to level four rather than as a gap in the path.
