Indoor navigation vs GPS: why satellites stop at the door

Updated 2026-11-24 · 8 min read · Indoor Wayfinding Solution engineering

Satellite positioning reaches a phone in open sky and stops at the building envelope. Indoors you need a venue-installed layer, BLE at 1-3 m, UWB at 0.1-0.3 m or Wi-Fi at 3-8 m, with a multi-floor map and a routing engine. A satellite fix tells you where the building is, never where you are inside it.

  • Concrete and coated glass cut the signal by tens of decibels.
  • Indoor GPS is not a product category. Name the signal and the accuracy instead.
  • Floor determination is a separate problem from horizontal positioning.
  • BLE at 1-3 m is the usual default for public wayfinding; UWB needs dense anchors.
  • Single-floor venues with one entrance often need only signage and a directory.

What does GPS actually deliver?

GPS is a constellation of satellites broadcasting timing signals on a fixed schedule. A receiver hearing four or more can solve for latitude, longitude, altitude and a clock correction, and that arithmetic is the position a phone reports. In open sky a modern handset reaches roughly 3 to 5 meters horizontally, better with differential correction, worse between tall buildings.

What the fix does not contain is anything about the inside of a structure. It does not know you are on the third floor, in a service corridor, or in the wrong wing. A shopping center and the office floor above it produce nearly the same two numbers. The system answers where on the earth, not where inside a building.

Why does the satellite signal fail indoors?

Signals arrive at 1.2 to 1.6 GHz with received power close to the noise floor. Concrete, brick, metal cladding, low-emissivity glass and foil-backed insulation take tens of decibels out of a link budget already priced on free-space loss, and what remains leaves too few satellites for a solution. A fix may still appear near a window or under a glass atrium, which is worse than none: the dot drifts by tens of meters.

Multipath is the second failure. Metal, water and dense fabric reflect the signal, and the receiver treats the delayed copy as a longer distance, so near entrances you get jumps and slow drift rather than a clean loss. Vertical geometry is the third: satellites sit above, vertical dilution of precision is poor, and altitude error runs several times the horizontal error, which is useless against a four-meter floor height.

Is indoor GPS a real product category?

The phrase appears in tenders and sales decks, and it is inaccurate. No satellite system provides indoor positioning as a service. What it describes is a venue that installed its own positioning layer and combined it with a map and a routing engine. Name the signal instead: BLE based indoor positioning, UWB real time locating, Wi-Fi based positioning, or simply indoor positioning and wayfinding.

The label costs money. A requirement written as indoor GPS invites a bid for a phone app with no site infrastructure, and the evaluation then compares two scopes as if they were one. Rewrite it as testable lines: horizontal accuracy at a stated percentile, correct floor at a stated rate, time to first indoor fix, coverage of step-free routes, and who owns the map after a refit.

Why is the floor the hardest part?

A horizontal coordinate on the wrong floor is worse than no answer: it sends a visitor confidently to a shop four levels up. Floor determination needs its own mechanism. A phone barometer reads roughly 0.12 hPa per meter, enough to separate floors but drifting with weather and building pressure control. Map matching rejects positions unreachable by continuous movement, which stops a route jumping through a slab. Floor-tagged beacons at stairwells, escalator landings and lift lobbies confirm the floor as a visitor passes.

Production systems combine all three. Horizontal position comes from BLE or Wi-Fi, floor from a barometer filtered against recent beacons and constrained by map topology. Lifts need explicit handling: a ride produces no horizontal movement and several floors of change, so the system must observe the transition rather than interpolate through it. UWB anchors at different heights resolve floor directly, at the price of a denser network.

How does outdoor-to-indoor handover work in practice?

The seam is the entrance, and that is where handover fails. A working implementation draws the venue boundary as a geofence on the outdoor map. The app navigates with satellite positioning until the visitor is within a set radius of a mapped entrance, then switches to the venue layer, anchoring the first indoor fix on a beacon at the door or a Wi-Fi RTT measurement inside the threshold. One instruction list follows, with a defined switch point.

Failures are predictable. An entrance geofenced too generously hands over while the visitor is still on a car park deck, where no indoor fix exists. Several entrances collapsing onto one map point destroy the last instruction. Differing projections produce a jump at the door, so both models must share a reference. Visitors arriving from a basement have no fix at all and need a stated starting point or a QR code at the stair head.

When is GPS plus a map genuinely enough?

Sometimes the honest answer is that you do not need indoor positioning, and a supplier who will not say so is selling. A single floor under a few thousand square meters with one entrance, a directory and a static map may answer every real question. An outdoor campus is the same: satellite routing covers the walk between buildings, and positioning earns its place only inside one.

The test is not floor area but the number of decisions a first-time visitor makes. Count floors, entrances and destinations. If they must choose between two lift banks, cross a bridge, or find a step-free route to a clinic, a real map with positioning pays for itself. If the route is one corridor among single-digit destinations, spend on signage first.

What should be in your decision checklist?

A short list separates a specification from a wish. Work through it before asking for a proposal, and keep the answers in the tender so every bidder prices the same scope.

  • Floor count, and whether any destination sits below a covered deck.
  • Entrance count, including underground parking links and staff doors.
  • The accuracy the task needs, not the best available: 3 m finds a shop, 0.3 m an asset.
  • Vertical nodes to model: lifts, escalators, stairs, bridges, fire doors.
  • Who edits plans and tenant points when a unit changes hands.
  • Whether step-free alternatives are the default on public routes.
  • Scope lines in the quote: hardware, install, calibration, content, support.

Ask a specific question

Does satellite positioning work at all inside a building?

Near a window, under a glass roof or on a car park top deck it often does, and that is the problem. The fix is real but drifts by tens of meters because the receiver hears reflections, and altitude error cannot resolve a floor. Treat any indoor satellite fix as unreliable and let the venue layer take over.

How accurate does indoor navigation need to be?

It depends on the decision the visitor makes. To say which side of a corridor to walk down, 3 to 5 meters is enough. To point at a specific bay or an asset on a shelf, 1 meter or better. Ask for accuracy per task and state whether the figure is horizontal or three dimensional.

Can Wi-Fi replace installed beacons?

Sometimes. Wi-Fi positioning usually lands in the 3 to 8 meter band, enough for corridor level guidance, not for bay level. It avoids new hardware but moves cost into a calibration survey that repeats when access points or fit-out change. Dense Wi-Fi and modest accuracy needs suit it.

Do visitors have to install an app?

No. A venue can publish a link or QR code at the entrance that opens a browser map and route, and pair it with directory kiosks for visitors who install nothing. An app gives a smoother experience and features such as location sharing, at the cost of the install step.

What fails most often at the entrance?

Handover. Entrances are geofenced too loosely, several doors collapse onto one map point, or the outdoor and indoor models do not share a projection, so the dot jumps at the threshold. Walk every entrance during acceptance, including car park and service doors, not only the main one.
