Indoor Navigation Guide: How It Works and What It Costs

Updated 2026-11-24 · 9 min read · Indoor Wayfinding Solution engineering

Indoor navigation combines four layers: a multi-floor map, a positioning method, a routing engine and a presentation layer. Beacons, ultra-wideband or Wi-Fi produce the position, the walk graph produces the route, and the app, kiosk or AR view presents it. Cost follows area, floor count, points of interest and integrations rather than the screen.

What is indoor navigation, and what does it do that a static map cannot?

An indoor map tells a visitor where things are. Indoor navigation tells a visitor what to do next: turn left at the escalator, take the lift to level three, the clinic is the fourth door on the right. The difference is a live position plus a route that is recalculated as the visitor moves, so the instruction stays true at the moment it is read.

For a venue this changes three things. Visitors stop queuing at reception for directions, so staff handle fewer repeated questions. Destinations on upper floors and in back corridors get found, which matters to tenants who pay for visibility. Every search, route and arrival also becomes a record you can read afterwards, which is where people counting, heatmaps and popular-destination reports come from.

The term indoor GPS still appears in tenders. Satellite positioning does not work inside a steel and concrete building because the signal arrives too weak to fix a position, so every indoor system is built from something else: beacons, ultra-wideband anchors, Wi-Fi, or a combination of these with map matching. If a proposal promises GPS indoors, ask which sensor produces the position and where it was tested.

What are the four layers of an indoor navigation system?

Every indoor navigation product is a stack of four layers, and each layer is scoped, built and tested separately. Confusing them is the most common reason a project underdelivers: a venue buys a handsome 3D model, expects directions, and finds there is no walkable graph underneath the drawing. The map layer holds geometry: floor outlines, walls, units, doors, lifts, stairs, escalators and points of interest. The positioning layer decides where the visitor is, including which floor. The routing layer holds the walk graph and the rules for choosing a path. The presentation layer is what the visitor touches: a kiosk in the lobby, a phone app, a mini program, an augmented reality camera view, or a shared location link.

  • Map: 3D geometry, floors, units, doors, vertical connections and points of interest.
  • Positioning: the sensor stack that turns radio signals into coordinates and a floor.
  • Routing: the walk graph plus the engine that computes fastest, step free or accessible paths.
  • Presentation: kiosk, app, AR overlay, directory, and the admin tools behind them.

Layers fail in different ways, which is why acceptance testing has to touch all four rather than the screen alone.

Which positioning method fits which building?

Accuracy figures are quoted for horizontal position, and they assume reasonable conditions. The same beacon network that holds one to three meters in a wide atrium can drift at the end of a tiled corridor where signal reflects off glass and metal. Treat a published range as a starting point and ask what the vendor measured, where, and with which handset.

The choice is commercial as much as technical. Ultra-wideband gives the tightest number and the highest cost per tracked object, which is why it usually appears where assets or staff are tracked rather than where a visitor simply needs a shop name. Beacons suit visitor wayfinding because each unit costs little, lasts years on a battery, and mounts on a ceiling without a power run.

How does the route graph turn a map into directions?

A route is computed on a graph, not on the drawing. The graph is a set of nodes joined by edges: corridor center lines, door thresholds, the mouth of an escalator, the inside of a lift car, the landing on the next floor. Each edge carries attributes such as width, step count, whether it is step free, and whether it is currently open.

Points of interest attach to the graph rather than floating over it. A shop door is a node and the shop is an attribute on that node, which is what lets a tenant rename without touching geometry, and what keeps routing correct when a large unit is subdivided into three. Node spacing is a design decision with visible consequences. Nodes placed too far apart on a long corridor produce routes that cut corners in the 3D view and instructions that arrive after the junction. Nodes placed by measurement at every decision point produce instructions that land where the visitor is standing.

  • Door nodes carry the access rule: open to visitors, staff only, alarmed exit.
  • Vertical edges record lift, escalator or stair so an access profile can filter them.
  • Points of interest attach to a door node, so the route end is correct by construction.

How does routing work across floors and between buildings?

Floors connect through vertical edges. A lift shaft is one edge with a travel cost, stairs and escalators are separate edges with different costs and different accessibility flags. The engine weights them so an able visitor gets the quickest path while a visitor using a wheelchair gets the shortest step free path even when that costs an extra few minutes.

Multi-floor routing supports up to 20 levels in one venue, which covers most mixed use complexes including basement parking. Cross-building routing joins two graphs through an outdoor segment, and the same mechanism carries the outdoor to indoor handover at a campus entrance, so a visitor arriving from a car park is not dropped at the door and asked to start again.

Parking adds its own vertical logic. A route to a parked car can begin with a plate search at a kiosk, then run to the lift, out of the lift on the parking level, and along the bay aisle to the space. Reverse car finding is the same graph walked backwards with a different starting point. Vertical edges make that route possible, and without them the bay is a place the system can name but cannot reach.

How does accessible routing work in practice?

Accessible routing is a filter over the graph plus a set of attributes that must be maintained. A step free profile excludes stairs and escalators where a lift serves the same pair of levels, prefers routes through doors wide enough for a wheelchair, and can route to an accessible toilet or a lowered counter when those points are modeled. The maintenance side is where venues slip. A lift going out of service breaks a step free route that looked correct at handover, so the platform needs a way to mark that lift unavailable and reroute at once. The same applies to a corridor closed for renovation, a fire door that is normally locked, and temporary hoarding around a retail unit.

Treat availability as live data, not as a drawing edit. A route that is geometrically right and operationally wrong is worse than a route that takes the long way round, because the visitor only discovers the problem after committing to it. A step free profile that ignores live lift state is a drawing of accessibility rather than a working route for the person who needs it.

  • Model every lift as a named vertical edge with an availability state.
  • Record door width and step free status for each threshold on the graph.
  • Give the venue a way to close an area without waiting for a survey visit.

What does a project involve, week by week?

Timing follows the same shape on most projects. Survey takes 3 to 5 days for a building of 50,000 to 100,000 square meters, assuming the site is accessible during business hours. Map production takes 2 to 3 weeks, platform setup and integration take 2 to 4 weeks, and on site calibration with training takes 1 week. Phased delivery lands at 6 to 10 weeks in total. All of these are typical figures, confirmed after survey.

Integration is the variable that moves the end date. A venue that needs only kiosks and a web admin can sit at the low end. A venue that wants the map inside an existing app, tied to a membership system and a parking barrier, needs the high end, because each integration has a review cycle on the other side that you do not control. Ask which integrations are in scope before you agree a date.

  • Survey, 3 to 5 days for 50,000 to 100,000 square meters.
  • Map production, 2 to 3 weeks including points of interest.
  • Platform setup and integration, 2 to 4 weeks depending on interfaces.
  • On site calibration and training, 1 week.
  • Total, 6 to 10 weeks phased, confirmed after survey.

What drives the cost of an indoor navigation project?

Cost follows quantity, and quantity follows four dimensions: gross area, floor count, number of points of interest, and number of integrations. Nothing in the budget scales with visitor numbers, which is why an accurate scope at tender stage matters more than a low headline rate that omits half the work. Area drives survey days and map production. Floor count drives vertical edges, calibration time per floor and usually beacon count. Points of interest drive tenant data work, the least predictable line in most budgets because it depends on the quality of the tenant list you can supply. Integrations drive engineering hours and a testing window you do not own.

Positioning hardware is the line procurement teams study first, and it is rarely where most of the money goes. A venue that specifies ultra-wideband for visitors will spend more on anchors and tags than a beacon venue spends on beacons, for an accuracy improvement most visitors cannot perceive. We say so when the requirement does not justify the method. We quote from a scope rather than a rate card, and return a budgetary proposal within 24 hours of receiving floor plans and a floor count.

Language and content load is the quiet multiplier. Each language is a separate content set covering tenant names, categories, event banners and directory text, and each set is maintained separately afterwards. A four language venue carries four times the content work, which is a cost line even when the software license does not change. Add the languages to the content budget before it is fixed, not after.

What tends to fail in the first year, and how is it caught?

The failures that surface after launch are rarely the ones the tender discussed. Beacon batteries reach the end of their cycle in clusters because they were installed in clusters, so a floor loses signal in a corridor rather than everywhere at once. A tenant renames, and the directory keeps routing visitors to the old name because nobody owns the content. A renovation closes a corridor, and the graph still sends people through it.

All three are operational rather than technical, and all three are visible in month three if anyone looks. A beacon health view, a change log for points of interest, and a named owner inside the venue cover most of it. Put those three items in the handover pack and the first year stops being a series of surprises. None of the three needs engineering work after handover, only routine attention from someone whose job includes it.

  • Beacon health and battery state visible in the admin, not discovered by a visitor.
  • A named venue owner for directory content, with a monthly review.
  • A change log so a closed area or a renamed tenant is traceable to a date.

Which questions should you ask before you select a system?

Eight questions separate proposals that will hold up from proposals that read well. Ask them in writing and compare the answers side by side rather than comparing totals, because the answers show what the vendor expects to do and the total shows only what it expects to charge. Send the list before the site visit, so answers are prepared rather than improvised in the meeting.

Selection checklist for an indoor navigation system
QuestionWhat a usable answer contains
What is measured at acceptance?A written target per floor, a named test route, a pass or fail rule.
Who owns the map data?A clause naming the venue as owner, with export in an open format.
How are tenant changes handled?An editor the venue can use, or a change process with a stated turnaround.
Which positioning method, and why?A named method, units per floor, and accuracy measured on site.
What happens when a lift fails?A way to mark it unavailable and reroute without a support ticket.
Which integrations are in scope?A named list, an owner on each side, a test window.
What does support include?Response target, coverage hours, and whether updates are included.
How is the kiosk fleet managed?Server capacity per terminal count and a remote update path.

How do you judge a vendor proposal?

Judge a vendor on what it will let you measure before you pay. A serious proposal names the positioning method, states a test route, and accepts a pass or fail rule tied to that route. A proposal that offers a general accuracy figure with no test route is telling you the number came from a specification sheet rather than from a building.

Second, look at the map editing path. Tenant churn is continuous in retail and frequent in offices, so a venue that must raise a ticket for every rename will let its map go stale within a year. Third, read the data ownership clause and the export format, because the map is a long lived asset and the platform is not.

Finally, check that the vendor separates capability from track record. We describe what the platform supports, such as multi-floor routing across up to 20 levels or one terminal server managing up to 100 directory terminals, and we treat schedules and accuracy as site dependent until a survey confirms them. A proposal that cannot draw that line is usually written from a template.

Ask a specific question

How accurate is indoor navigation on a phone?

Typical horizontal accuracy is 1 to 3 meters with beacons, 1 to 2 meters with a hybrid of beacons, Wi-Fi and map matching, 3 to 8 meters on Wi-Fi alone, and 0.1 to 0.3 meters with ultra-wideband anchors and tags. Values are site dependent and should be re-measured on site during calibration, especially in long corridors.

Do visitors need to install an app?

No. The same map can be delivered as a web link from a QR poster, inside a mini program, inside the venue's own app through an SDK, or on a directory kiosk in the lobby. Most venues run two of these, a kiosk for arrivals and a phone route for the walk, and manage the content once for both.

How long does an indoor navigation project take?

A typical project runs 6 to 10 weeks in phases: survey 3 to 5 days for 50,000 to 100,000 square meters, map production 2 to 3 weeks, platform setup and integration 2 to 4 weeks, calibration and training 1 week. Integration scope is the main variable, and the schedule is confirmed after survey.

Can indoor navigation work without beacons?

It can where the building already has dense Wi-Fi coverage, but accuracy falls to roughly 3 to 8 meters and floor detection becomes less reliable. For corridors, lobby junctions and multi-floor routing, beacons at low density plus map matching hold a steadier position for a smaller hardware spend.

Can the map be updated when tenants change?

Yes, and the mechanism matters more than the promise. A map editor lets venue staff rename a unit, move a point of interest or recategorise a tenant without code changes. Where the venue prefers not to edit, a change process with a stated turnaround is the alternative, and it should appear in the contract.

What about privacy in an indoor positioning system?

We do not sell surveillance. The visitor position exists to compute a route, and people counting works from aggregated search, route and sensor data rather than from identifying individuals. Staff positioning covers consenting staff devices or badges during shifts for dispatch, patrol and emergency response, with no camera based identity tracking and no biometric identification.
