Wayfinding Project Cost: How Budgets Are Actually Built

Updated 2026-11-24 · 9 min read · Indoor Wayfinding Solution engineering

A wayfinding budget is built from six blocks: survey and map production, positioning hardware, kiosks and displays, platform license, integration and customization, deployment and training, plus annual support. Area drives survey and mapping, floor count drives vertical work, points of interest drive tenant data, and integrations drive engineering hours.

What are the six cost blocks in a wayfinding budget?

Every proposal, from any supplier including us, reduces to the same six blocks plus annual support. Reading a quote block by block is the fastest way to find what was left out, because the omissions cluster in two places: calibration and data. Both are invisible in a demonstration and both are charged later. Six lines should appear separately even when the total is fixed, because the split shows what the vendor expects to spend effort on. A quote where survey is one line and integration is absent is telling you that integration is either out of scope or unpriced, and an unpriced interface becomes a variation.

The block order below is deliberate: it follows the order in which money is committed, from the survey that fixes the scope, through hardware and terminals that arrive on site, to the license and support that recur. A budget built in that order is easy to audit at renewal, when the question is not what the system cost but what it costs to keep.

  • Survey and map production: measured geometry, points of interest, walk graph.
  • Positioning hardware: beacons, gateways, ultra-wideband anchors and tags.
  • Kiosks and displays: terminals, enclosures, mounting, content.
  • Platform license: hosted, private cloud or on premise, per venue.
  • Integration and customization: named interfaces, screens, languages, branding.
  • Deployment and training: calibration, staff training, documentation.
  • Annual support, the seventh line: response target, updates, extended coverage.

How are survey and map production scoped?

This block is driven by area and by the state of the drawings. Survey runs 3 to 5 days for 50,000 to 100,000 square meters, and map production runs 2 to 3 weeks on top, which sets the floor for the whole schedule. Points of interest add time in proportion to the quality of the tenant list you can supply.

Two venues of the same area do not cost the same to map. A regular retail grid with current plans is faster than a hospital with wings added in three phases and no drawings that match. Ask what happens when the drawings are wrong, because on older buildings they usually are and the survey absorbs the difference in the field.

The line also carries the walk graph, which is the part most easily under-scoped. A vendor that quotes map production but leaves the graph for a later phase has quoted a drawing, not a navigation system, and the visitor will find out on the first route. Ask which deliverable contains the graph, and whether nodes at doorways are measured on site or estimated from the plan.

What drives positioning hardware quantity?

Hardware quantity is a function of floor area and mounting conditions, not of visitor numbers. Ceiling height, ceiling material and the density of partitions all change the count, so a figure derived from a rate per square meter without a site visit is an estimate and should be labeled as one. The method changes the order of magnitude more than the density does. Beacons cost little per unit and run for years on a battery, so a floor can carry a comfortable density without a large spend. Ultra-wideband anchors and per-object tags are priced differently, and the tag count grows with every person or asset you want to locate. Specify ultra-wideband where sub-meter accuracy is genuinely required, and expect the line to be several times the beacon equivalent.

Budget the installation labor as a separate consideration even when it sits inside the hardware line. Beacons on a ceiling mount need a lift and an hour, while gateways that draw mains power need a cable run and an electrician. That difference is invisible in a unit count. Ask for the installation line separately, because it is the one most often left out of a hardware comparison.

How do kiosks and displays enter the budget?

Kiosks are priced per unit and the unit price varies with screen size, enclosure, mounting method and whether a reader for staff check-in is included. The terminal server is priced separately and one server can manage up to 100 directory terminals, which means the server line stays flat while the terminal line scales. The most common budgeting error is over-ordering units. Model the count from footfall paths and decision points, not from floor area. Six terminals at the right lobbies and junctions do more than twenty spread evenly along a concourse, and they cost less to power, network and maintain.

Site services belong in this block because somebody has to provide them. Each position needs power, a network drop and a fixing method that suits the floor finish. Where those do not exist, the cost moves into electrical and civil work, which typically sits outside a wayfinding quote and is discovered on site. An electrical survey before the order is cheaper than a change order after delivery.

How is the platform license structured, and what does annual support add?

Licensing usually follows the deployment model. A hosted subscription is priced per venue and per term, a private cloud deployment carries a license plus an infrastructure line that the venue or the vendor operates, and an on premise deployment moves the infrastructure entirely to the venue. All three are available, and the choice is as much about IT policy as about budget.

Read the license on three points: what counts as a venue, what happens when you add a second building on the same site, and whether the license survives a change of operator. The third point matters in retail and hospitality, where management contracts change and the incoming operator inherits a system it did not buy. Annual support is a separate line and should be quoted as one. The standard term includes 12 months of warranty on supplied hardware and software updates during the support term, with extended support available as an option. Ask what the response target is, which hours it covers, and whether a failed beacon is replaced under the term or charged.

What do integration, customization, deployment and training add?

Integration is priced per interface, and two interfaces that look similar can differ by a wide margin. A parking plate lookup against a defined interface is a bounded piece of work. A membership system with no documented API is a discovery project, and the discovery is billable whether or not the integration ships. The named interfaces we work with cover venue apps and mini programs, CRM and membership, point of sale, parking systems with plate recognition and barriers, digital signage content management, access control, fire and emergency systems, hotel property systems, and read-only directory data from hospital information systems. Each one has an owner on the other side and a test window you do not control, which is why integration drives the end date more than any other block.

Customization covers the parts that are specific to your venue: new screen layouts, additional languages, branding, and workflow changes in the admin. Deployment and training covers on site calibration, which runs about a week in a typical scope, plus training for administrators and front of house staff, and the documentation that lets the venue operate without calling support for routine changes.

What scales with area, with floors, with points of interest, with integrations?

Four dimensions explain most of the variance between two quotes for venues that look similar on paper. When a comparison does not make sense, one of these four is usually being counted differently on each side. Area is the dimension most often over-weighted in comparisons, because it is the only one a buyer can read from a floor plan without help. Floor count and integration count change the total more sharply per unit: a building with the same area on four floors instead of eight carries roughly half the vertical work, and a project with one integration carries a fraction of the testing of a project with four.

Per square meter or per floor: which commercial model fits?

A per square meter model is easy to compare and it suits large, regular retail floors where beacon density is roughly uniform and tenants are laid out on a grid. Its weakness is that it treats a warehouse sized open hall and a subdivided office floor as equivalents, even though the second needs more graph work per square meter. A per floor model suits offices, hospitals and mixed use towers, where area per floor is modest and the difficult work is vertical: lift logic, per floor calibration and floor detection. A third model prices a fixed platform fee plus per floor mapping plus hardware per unit, which is closer to how the work is actually done and easier to adjust when one floor changes.

Market maturity changes the number in three ways. Where comparable projects are common, survey crews and map production capacity are competitive, and vendors have reusable workflows for familiar building types. Where indoor mapping is newer, the same scope carries more survey time, more calibration iteration, and a larger share of the budget in engineering rather than hardware. Local labor rates and the availability of installation crews move the hardware line as much as the software line does. Compare proposals block by block across markets rather than comparing totals.

What do three project scopes look like?

Most projects we quote fall into one of three shapes, and naming the shape early keeps the budget honest. A pilot covers one floor or one zone, one positioning method, a single kiosk or a web route, and no integration. It exists to prove accuracy and adoption before a full commitment, and it should be priced so that its survey and map work carries forward rather than being thrown away.

A single venue scope covers the whole building with multi-floor routing, kiosks at the main decision points, one or two integrations and training. This is the shape most malls and hospitals buy. A portfolio scope covers several venues on one platform, with per venue content management, integrations repeated per venue, and central administration, and it is the only shape where platform economics improve with volume.

Pilot pricing deserves one rule: the surveyed area must be reusable. If a pilot quote covers a zone but describes its survey as standalone, you pay for the same corridors twice, and the saving on the pilot disappears in phase two. Ask explicitly whether the pilot survey feeds the full venue map, and put the answer in the phase two scope.

Which five procurement traps should you check?

Five traps account for most of the gap between a signed price and a paid one. None of them appears as a high rate. Each appears as a missing line, which is why comparing totals hides all five: the bid that leaves out calibration and the bid that includes it look like two prices for the same building rather than two different scopes.

  • The cheap map: geometry quoted without a walk graph, so routing arrives as a later phase.
  • Missing calibration: accuracy promised on paper, with per floor tuning priced as an extra.
  • Kiosk without network: terminals quoted without power, cabling or mounting at each position.
  • No data ownership clause: the map cannot be exported, so renewal has no competition.
  • Support outside the contract: response times agreed verbally, with no coverage hours written.

Each trap is cheap at signature and expensive later: a map without a graph cannot route, and a venue without an export cannot move.

How do you run a two-stage tender?

Stage one is capability and scope, with no price. Ask each vendor for the positioning method it proposes and why, the survey method, the map accuracy it will accept, a reference building type it has worked in, and the export format. Score those answers, shortlist three, and stop. Vendors that cannot answer without pricing are usually quoting from a template.

Stage two is a site visit, a confirmed scope, and a priced worksheet on the six block structure with the same line names for every bidder. Add the acceptance test route to the same package, because a bidder that sees the test list prices calibration honestly rather than optimistically. Evaluate on the worksheet and the answers, not on the total. Weight the blocks that carry operational risk for you, which in most venues are calibration, data ownership and support, and treat a bid that is materially below the others as a scope question rather than a bargain. Ask which line it left out before you assume it is competitive.

Ask a specific question

Why can two quotes for the same building differ so much?

Usually because they count different things. One may price map production without the walk graph, another may exclude per floor calibration, a third may quote kiosks without site services. Compare the six blocks line by line and the difference normally resolves into a missing scope rather than a different rate.

Is per square meter pricing cheaper?

Not reliably. It is easier to compare and it suits large open retail floors, but it under-prices subdivided office floors where graph and calibration work scales with rooms rather than area. A fixed platform fee plus per floor mapping plus hardware per unit usually tracks the real work more closely.

What is usually missing from a cheap quote?

Calibration, site services at kiosk positions, the data ownership and export clause, and a written support commitment. Each is small at signature and large afterwards. Ask for those four items in writing before comparing prices, and treat a refusal to include them as a scope statement.

How should a budget be phased across years?

Phase one is survey, map production and a pilot zone, which proves accuracy and adoption on a limited footprint. Phase two adds the remaining floors, kiosks and integrations once the pilot passes acceptance. Phase three covers support and content operations. Structuring it this way keeps each commitment testable and lets the survey work carry forward.

Do we keep the map if we change platform later?

Only if the contract says so. Ask for a clause naming the venue as owner of the geometry, the walk graph and the beacon register, with export in an open exchange format at any time. Without that clause the map is a service output, and the cost of moving becomes a resurvey.

How quickly can we get a budgetary proposal?

We return a budgetary proposal within 24 hours of receiving floor plans and a floor count, with the six blocks shown separately and the survey schedule marked as typical until confirmed on site. That is enough to test a business case before committing to a full site survey.
