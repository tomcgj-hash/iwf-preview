Privacy in indoor positioning: what a venue signs up to

Updated 2026-11-24 · 8 min read · Indoor Wayfinding Solution engineering

An indoor system collects search terms, routes, dwell times, plates and device or badge identifiers. Some is personal data; some is only personal while a key can re-link it. A venue needs a purpose, a retention period and an access rule per data type, staff location treated as employee monitoring, and a contract naming fields and deletion.

Takeaways

  • Indoor systems collect more than footfall: search terms, routes, dwell and plates.
  • Pseudonymisation is not anonymisation. If a key exists, the data is still personal.
  • Aggregate at collection if you only want footfall. That removes the question.
  • Staff positioning is employee monitoring and needs proportionality and shift limits.
  • Set retention per data type, and test deletion during acceptance.

What does an indoor positioning system actually collect?

The list is longer than most venues expect. Kiosk logs record what someone typed and when. Navigation sessions record origin and destination as point of interest identifiers, with timestamps. Zone events record entry and exit at a drawn boundary, which yields dwell time. A reverse car finder records a plate. Staff positioning adds a badge identifier, and once that badge belongs to a named employee, so does every position on it.

Two categories sit in that list. One is what a session needs to give guidance now: a position, a floor, a destination. It is transient. The other is the record that remains, journeys that can be analyzed, exported or joined with a loyalty account or a till receipt. The first is nearly unavoidable. The second is a decision that needs a purpose and a retention period.

What counts as personal data under GDPR-style rules?

A device identifier is personal data when it can be linked to an identifiable person, and that test is broad. A visitor signed into a venue app is clearly identifiable. A randomised Bluetooth address is harder, but the address plus a time-stamped path plus a purchase at 14:12 can single out one person. What matters is what can be joined to the identifier, and by whom.

Two tests decide the regime. Linkability: can the record be tied to a person by the venue, the platform operator, or an integration pulling in a member number. Purpose: is there a basis, and is collection limited to what it needs. Footfall counted at collection, with the session dropped, usually falls outside the definition. A record that persists across a visit does not.

Can indoor positional data be anonymised?

Anonymisation means the data can no longer be attributed to a person by any reasonably likely means. Pseudonymisation replaces the identifier with a key, and the key still exists. Most claims of anonymous positional data are the second dressed as the first. If a mapping table sits on the platform side, the data stays personal for whoever holds the key.

One robust route exists: aggregate at collection. If a kiosk or sensor increments a zone counter and stores no session, there is nothing to re-identify. Device-side navigation cannot be fully anonymous while the app must know where the phone is to route it. What you can do is minimise: short-lived session tokens, no cross-session identifier, no account link unless a feature needs one, no free-text field.

How do cameras, device positioning and staff badges differ?

Three sensing methods are discussed as interchangeable. They are not, and each carries a different exposure. Read the columns together: the method that looks cheapest to install often carries the heaviest notice and retention burden.

Where is the line between footfall and tracking a person?

The line is drawn by the question. Footfall asks how many people were in a zone at 14:00, compared with last Tuesday. Per-person tracking asks where this visitor came from, where they went next and whether they bought. The first needs counters that hold no session. The second needs an individual record and a purpose that survives a necessity test. Improving the experience is a real goal, not a sufficient purpose.

Design signals tell you which you are buying. A stable identifier that outlives a session, path replay across one visit, cross-visit linking, joins with loyalty or point of sale data, or raw track export are all per-person features. Each can be legitimate, and each needs its own justification, notice line and retention rule.

Does staff positioning count as employee monitoring?

Yes, in most jurisdictions, and treat it that way from the start. Consent is weak here even with a signed form, given the employer-employee imbalance. The basis is usually legitimate interest or a legal obligation, either of which needs proportionality: the narrowest coverage that meets the purpose, during shifts only, with the purpose written down. Dispatch, patrol compliance, lone worker safety and emergency mustering are defensible. Continuous performance scoring is not.

In several countries employee representatives must be involved before monitoring starts, in some as a precondition. Measures that make deployment defensible: publish what is collected and who can see it, switch the tag off outside shifts, keep patrol statistics at route and check-in level rather than minute by minute, log who opens history tracks, and state in writing that camera based identity tracking and biometric identification are excluded.

How long should data be kept, and who may see it?

Retention belongs per data type. One company-wide period is either too long for one table or too short for another. The pattern: short for continuous data, longer for aggregates, shortest for anything linkable outside the venue.

Data typePurposeSuggested retentionRisk if kept longer

Route session historyJourney analytics, congestion, dead ends30 to 90 days, aggregated before storageGradual reconstruction of individual journeys
Search termsDestination demand, language coverage30 days raw, or aggregated at collectionTyped personal data accumulates in logs
Zone countersFootfall and dwell reporting12 to 24 months, aggregatedLow if aggregated, high if a session key survives
Staff position historyDispatch, patrol compliance, incidents30 to 90 days, with access loggedPerformance surveillance by another name
Plate lookupsReverse car finding, bay reservationHours to days after the vehicle exitsPlates identify a person past the purpose

A scheduled deletion job is worth more than a retention clause. Test it with a record older than the stated period.

What should the contract and the public notice contain?

A processing agreement is only useful if it names fields. Generic language about protecting personal data will not tell you what happens to a plate number after the vehicle leaves, or whether search terms are joined to a member record.

The public notice can be short and still honest. One line each for who runs the system, what is collected and why, what is not collected (no facial recognition, no camera based identity tracking, no biometric identification), staff location with its purpose and shift limits, retention, sharing, and rights. Publish it at the app entry and on the kiosk, and date the version.

  • Name controller and processor per data category, with kiosk counting and app analytics separate.
  • List sub-processors, with notice and a right to object before any change.
  • State the storage region and whether any transfer leaves it.
  • Set retention per data type, with deletion verified rather than promised.
  • Name the security measures: encryption, key custody, admin multi-factor, audit logs.
  • Set a breach notification window shorter than the statutory maximum.
  • State whether venue data may train or improve the supplier product. Default: no.
  • Require return and deletion at contract end, including backups and derived analytics.

Ask a specific question

Is a device MAC address personal data?

Usually yes when combined with anything else. A randomised address alone is weak evidence of identity, but with a time-stamped path and a till transaction it can single out one person. Assume the joined record is personal and set retention for it.

Do we need consent for footfall counting?

Not usually, if counting is genuinely aggregated at collection and no individual record is stored. Aggregated counts are commonly handled under legitimate interest with a notice at the entrance. The moment a session key, a track or an identifier survives, the analysis moves toward personal data.

Can we use indoor positioning without a privacy notice?

No. Even where the basis is legitimate interest rather than consent, a notice has to explain what is collected, why, how long it is kept and how to exercise rights. Put a short version where collection starts, at the app entry and on the kiosk, and link the full notice.

Can we track staff without involving a works council?

In several countries you cannot. Where employee representatives exist they must usually be consulted before monitoring starts, and in some jurisdictions agreement is a precondition. Treat that as a project dependency: it shapes scope, coverage hours and reported statistics, and changing those later reopens the discussion.

How do we handle a deletion request from a visitor?

You have to find the record first, which is why identifiers and retention matter. If sessions are pseudonymous and short-lived there is often nothing to delete, and saying so is a valid answer. If an account link exists, the record is identifiable and must be found and erased within the statutory window, including analytics exports.

Does using the existing Wi-Fi network change the position?

It can widen it. A venue reading Wi-Fi association data may be processing guest and staff identifiers on a network run for other purposes, so the purpose and notice must cover the new use. Calibration surveys also collect walking traces, which are positional data even when unpublished.
