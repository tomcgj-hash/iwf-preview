Indoor Map Accuracy: What Precision Really Means

Updated 2026-11-24 · 8 min read · Indoor Wayfinding Solution engineering

Indoor map accuracy is how closely drawn geometry matches the building. Positioning accuracy is how closely a reported position matches the visitor. A route fails when either number is wrong, or when the walk graph skips a door. Accept a floor only after measured checks on both, on the handsets visitors will use.

Takeaways

  • Map accuracy is measured in centimeters on geometry; positioning accuracy is measured in meters on people.
  • Door placement and walk graph topology cause more wrong destinations than positioning error does.
  • Tracing a floor plan inherits the plan's error; a control traverse keeps the trace honest.
  • Floor determination needs a lift lobby signal, a map matching rule, or both.
  • Maps go stale at the speed of the building, so editing access matters more than survey precision.
  • Acceptance needs a written route, a checkpoint list and a pass or fail rule, agreed before survey.

Why do two different accuracy numbers appear in every project?

A project manager hears two accuracy figures and assumes one is a sales number. They measure different things. Map accuracy is the distance between a wall drawn on the plan and the wall in the building. Positioning accuracy is the distance between the dot on the screen and the visitor holding the phone. A route fails when either one is wrong, and a third element, the walk graph, can fail on its own.

That separation explains a common dispute. A vendor demonstrates positioning within two meters in the lobby, the venue accepts, and three weeks later staff report that directions send visitors to the wrong shop. The position was fine. The door sat a meter off on the drawing, or the graph attached the shop to the corridor node on the wrong side.

The practical consequence is that accuracy must be specified per layer, not once for the project. Write the target for geometry, the target for position, and the rule for door and floor correctness separately. A single number in a proposal usually refers to the layer the vendor finds easiest to demonstrate. A vendor that offers one figure for everything is either simplifying for a tender or has not measured a building of this shape.

What does cartographic accuracy mean on an indoor map?

Cartographic accuracy is expressed in centimeters and it applies to a list of features, not to a picture. Which features matter depends on what the venue does with the map. For routing, the critical ones are door openings, corridor widths, the position of vertical connections and unit boundaries. For a directory, they are the tenant name, the category and the door the tenant answers at.

An indoor map that looks right at a glance can be wrong in the ways that matter. A model built from photographs matches the photographs, not the building. A traced floor plan inherits the drafting error of the plan, and older plans carry renovations that were never redrawn. Accuracy therefore needs a stated feature list, a tolerance per feature and a measurement method, or nobody can test it.

One useful discipline is to separate what must be centimeter correct from what must only be approximately right. Door thresholds, lift lobbies and stair mouths belong in the first group because a route turns on them. Corridor center lines in a long straight run belong in the second, because half a meter of drift there changes nothing the visitor experiences.

A tolerance set that works for planning looks like this, and each figure is confirmed after survey: door openings within 20 cm, lift lobbies within 20 cm, unit boundaries within 50 cm, corridor center lines within 1 m, and floor to floor height within 10 cm. The pattern matters more than the exact numbers. Thresholds are tight because routes turn on them, and open runs are loose because nothing the visitor experiences depends on them.

Which errors actually ruin a route?

Six failures account for most escalations after go live, and only two of them are positioning accuracy problems in the usual sense. The others are data and topology faults that a demonstration will never reveal, because demonstrations run on the routes the vendor chose. How are indoor maps surveyed?

Four methods are used in practice, alone or in combination. The choice depends on the building, on which drawings exist, and on the tolerance the route actually needs. The accuracy values below are typical planning guidance, confirmed after survey. Most commercial buildings are mapped by combining methods: tracing for bulk geometry, laser measurement for doors and vertical connections, and a control traverse for the points that keep the trace honest. The combination keeps cost sensible because centimeter work is spent only where a route depends on it, rather than evenly across every square meter.

Survey logistics affect both cost and accuracy. A trading mall rarely allows a crew to close a corridor during the day, so measurement happens before opening or after closing, in shifts, and the schedule has to assume that. Hospitals need infection control clearance, and transport buildings need security escort. A survey quoted at three days assumes access, not just area, and access is the assumption most often broken.

How is the walk graph built, and why is it not the drawing?

The walk graph is built from the surveyed plan and it is where accuracy becomes navigation. Nodes sit on walkable surface, on a center line that leaves room for a person to pass. Edges connect nodes along corridors, through door thresholds, and onto lift, stair and escalator connections. None of this is visible on the rendered model, which is why it is easy to buy a map that looks finished and routes badly.

Two rules keep the graph honest. First, nodes at decision points are measured rather than estimated, because a node two meters past a junction produces an instruction to turn after the visitor has passed it. Second, a point of interest attaches to a door node rather than to a coordinate, so the final meters of every route are correct by construction rather than by luck.

The graph is also where service rules live: staff only doors, alarmed exits, one way corridors, areas closed after hours. Those rules change more often than geometry, so they belong in an editor with a change log, not in a survey deliverable. Validate the graph before the survey crew leaves. Walk each floor with the graph open on a tablet and follow the corridor center line: a node that sits inside a wall, an edge that crosses a planter, a door that opens inward when the graph assumes the opposite, all appear within a minute of walking the route. Finding them then costs an hour. Finding them at acceptance costs a return visit.

How is floor detection made reliable?

Floor is the failure that feels most broken to a visitor, because a route that starts one level up is wrong from the first step. Three signals are used, usually together, and each covers a weakness in the others. None of the three is reliable alone, and the cost of getting this right at design stage is a fraction of a corrective visit.

  • Barometer: relative pressure change detects a lift ride, but drifts with weather and air handling.
  • Map matching: position history constrained to walkable surface on one floor resolves most ambiguity.
  • Beacon floor identifier: one beacon at each lift lobby states the floor outright.
  • Handover rule: on arrival at a lobby, the identified floor overrides the inferred one.

How often do indoor maps need updating?

Maps go stale at the speed of the building, not at the speed of the project. Retail churn is continuous, hospitals reconfigure wards, offices subdivide floors, and transport buildings move zones between seasons. A map delivered in a six week project can be materially wrong within a year if nobody owns the content. Set a cadence that matches the venue: a monthly review of tenant data in retail, quarterly in office buildings, and event driven elsewhere, triggered by a fit out or a renovation. What matters more than the cadence is who does the work. A venue with an editor renames a tenant in minutes. A venue that must raise a ticket waits days for a change visitors notice immediately.

Not every change needs a survey. A tenant rename, a category change, a new point of interest behind an existing door and a closed corridor are all edits. What needs measured geometry is a new opening, a wall removed, a floor reconfigured or a lift added or removed, because those change the walkable surface itself. Keeping the two lists separate stops venues from over-ordering surveys and stops maps from drifting.

How do you test accuracy before acceptance?

Test in the building, on the handsets visitors will use, on routes that include the hard cases. Three tests cover most of the risk, and each produces a number rather than an opinion. The positioning test walks a measured corridor with checkpoints, recording error at each one and noting the conditions. The door test walks to ten destination doors across at least three floors and confirms the route ends at the correct opening rather than the correct unit. The floor test rides every lift connection in both directions and confirms the reported floor at each landing.

Conditions belong in the record. Note whether the test ran with visitors present, which handsets were used, and whether the floors were furnished, because a newly fitted out floor behaves differently from an empty shell. Comparing two rounds measured under different conditions is how venues end up arguing about a number rather than about the route. Write the conditions down next to the numbers, so a later comparison is about the route rather than about the weather.

  • Ten checkpoints per floor on a measured corridor, error recorded at each.
  • Ten destination doors, at least three floors, route end confirmed on foot.
  • Every connected lift pair, both directions, reported floor confirmed.
  • Run at handover, then again after two weeks of live use.

What does an acceptance test protocol look like?

A protocol that fits on one page gets run. Write it into the contract before the survey, because acceptance targets change what the survey has to measure. A survey done without them tends to produce a map that is visually complete and dimensionally approximate. The protocol needs five parts: the route list, the checkpoint schedule, the pass or fail rule per test, the rework window, and the sign off authority on the venue side. Keep the results table with the handover pack so the next phase, or the next vendor, starts from a baseline rather than from a demonstration.

  • Route list: chosen by the venue, covering each floor and each lift connection.
  • Checkpoint schedule: fixed positions, fixed measurement method, fixed handset.
  • Pass or fail rule: a stated tolerance, plus a rule for door and floor correctness.
  • Rework window: days allowed to correct a failing route before sign off.
  • Sign off: named venue authority, and the results table retained at handover.

What should the handover pack contain?

What you keep matters more than what you see at handover. A venue that receives a login and a finished model has bought a service. A venue that receives the assets has bought a map it can carry forward, and that changes the conversation at renewal. Ask for the export format in writing before signature. Source geometry, the walk graph and the beacon register are the three items that let another platform, or another phase, continue without remeasuring the building.

  • Source geometry and floor outlines in an open exchange format.
  • Walk graph export with node and edge attributes.
  • Beacon register listing every unit, its floor and its placement.
  • Calibration log and the acceptance test results table.
  • Admin credentials, integration documentation and a change procedure.

Ask a specific question

Is one meter of map accuracy enough for routing?

Usually yes for corridor geometry, and not enough for doors. A meter of drift in a long straight corridor changes nothing the visitor experiences, while the same drift at a doorway produces a route that ends at the wrong unit. Spend the precision on thresholds, lift lobbies and junctions, and accept looser tolerance in open runs.

Why does the route end at the wrong door?

Almost always because the door node was placed from the drawing rather than surveyed on site, or because a unit was subdivided after the map was made and the points of interest were not reattached. Both are fixed by measuring door openings at handover and by attaching points of interest to door nodes with a review process.

How accurate does floor detection need to be?

It needs to be right at lift lobbies, which is where floors change and where the risk of a slip is highest. There is no useful tolerance there: a route that starts one floor up is wrong immediately. A floor identifier beacon at each lobby, plus map matching elsewhere, is the lowest cost way to hold that standard.

Can a map be built from drone or satellite imagery?

Not for interiors. Overhead imagery cannot see inside a building, so indoor geometry comes from floor plans, laser measurement, a total station traverse or handheld scanning. Aerial capture is useful for the outdoor approach and site model that connect to the indoor map, and that outdoor segment is part of cross-building routing.

How often should accuracy be re-tested?

After any renovation, fit out or beacon replacement, and as an annual spot check on a sample of routes. Full acceptance is only needed again when a floor changes materially. Recording each round in the same results table keeps comparisons meaningful and shows whether drift is growing.
